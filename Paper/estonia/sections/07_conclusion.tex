\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper has constructed the first annual, internationally comparable series of Estonian GDP per capita for the years between the collapse of the Russian Empire and the end of the transition from Soviet rule, and has joined it to existing estimates to give a continuous series for 1913 and 1919--2022 in 1990 and 2011 international dollars. The reconstruction rests on a new 1938 benchmark obtained by a direct price comparison with Sweden, on a recalculated production-side series for 1919--40, and on a new series for the ESSR for 1937--95 built from physical output, transport and employment data in Soviet yearbooks and the Estonian archives and anchored to Bolotin's 1990 benchmark. The two new series were constructed from different sources, with different weights and from benchmarks half a century apart; where they overlap, in 1937--40, they agree within 4 per cent in every year, and the Soviet-period series meets a modern series derived from the 2011 ICP round within 1 per cent at the 1991 splice. The series supersedes the three partial estimates that preceded it, lowering Estonian income in the late Soviet years by about a quarter relative to the Maddison Project's NMP-based figures and raising it by a similar margin relative to the only earlier long series, and it is ready for incorporation into the Maddison Project Database.

Three findings stand out. First, Estonia's interwar growth was respectable but not exceptional: \gr{1919}{1929} per cent a year in the 1920s, when the country was recovering from war and the loss of its imperial market, and \gr{1929}{1938} per cent in the 1930s, when devaluation and a state-led industrial drive produced one of the fastest recoveries from the Depression in Europe. Second, Soviet rule brought two decades of rapid growth followed by two decades of stagnation. Income per head grew by \gr{1950}{1968} per cent a year in 1950--68, faster than in Finland or Western Europe, as investment directed from Moscow built an oil-shale, textile and engineering economy on an unusually literate labour force; it grew by \gr{1968}{1989} per cent a year in 1968--89, as the sources of extensive growth ran out and the system proved unable to replace them. Estonia's income relative to the Western core rose from two-thirds in 1950 to seven-tenths in 1968 and fell back to half by 1989. Within the Union, Estonia remained throughout what it had been at annexation, a republic between a fifth and a half richer than the average, neither converging on the Soviet mean nor pulling away from it. Third, the transition cut income per head by a third in four years, and recovery after 1995 was fast enough to carry Estonia, by 2022, back to the share of Western European income it had briefly reached in 1968.

The comparison with Finland, which gives the paper its title, needs to be restated. The two countries were close in 1913 and 1919, but by 1938 Estonia stood at three-quarters of the Finnish level, and almost half of the gap that separated them in 1989 had opened under independence, not occupation. The further divergence under Soviet rule was concentrated after 1968: in the preceding two decades Estonia had kept pace with Finland and in 1968 stood closer to it than at any time since 1919. Finland shows what institutions can do with a given endowment of human capital, but it does not show what Estonia would have become, because the Finnish path depended on resources and markets that Estonia did not have. The costs of Soviet rule were real, and the series locates them in the stagnation of 1968--89; measured against the Western core or the Western periphery rather than against Finland, whose growth in those decades was exceptional, they are smaller than the Finnish comparison implies, and they are not the costs of a Finnish miracle that Estonia was denied.

Several extensions are open. The 1938 benchmark's service sector, which rests on earnings and employment data, would benefit from a dedicated study of the kind now available for Lithuania and Latvia. A republican benchmark for the Soviet period built from the archives of the Estonian statistical administration, with Estonian rather than Union-wide prices, would test the level to which the volume indices are anchored. Expenditure-side cross-checks are feasible for a few interwar years. And the procedure followed here can be applied to the other non-Russian republics, whose national accounts remain the largest gap in the quantitative economic history of the Soviet Union.
