\section{Validation, limitations and sensitivity}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{The overlaps}

The reconstruction can be tested at two points: the interwar and Soviet-period series both cover 1937--40, and the Soviet-period and modern series, the latter backcast from the MPD's 2011 benchmark, both cover 1990--5 (Table~\ref{tab:overlaps}).

\input{../../Tables/estonia/T3_overlaps.tex}

In 1937--40 the two series differ by $-$1.6, $+$3.6, $-$3.0 and $-$0.3 per cent, all within the 5 per cent margin that Feinstein reserved for `firm' estimates, and not of one sign.\footnote{\autocite[21]{feinstein1972}.} The test is demanding. The two series share no sources, the one resting on the republic's own statistics and the other on ESSR yearbooks and post-war archives; no weights, one using the structure of 1938 and the other that of 1990; and no benchmark, one a bilateral price comparison with Sweden, the other a purchasing-power-parity study of the Soviet republics fifty-two years later. That the Soviet-period series, extrapolated backwards over half a century of war, collectivisation and forced industrialisation, arrives within 4 per cent of an independently measured level is the strongest evidence available that the net error of the extrapolation is small. It cannot exclude errors that happen to offset, such as an overstatement of growth from hidden inflation in trade turnover against an understatement from the index-number assumptions. But the two index-number biases discussed below, late base-year weights and fixed value-added weights, would both understate growth and so would both place the backcast 1937--40 level above the interwar series; the overlap therefore tests their combined size. A net understatement of growth of as little as 0.1 percentage point a year over 1940--90 would place the Soviet-period series 5 per cent above the interwar series in every overlap year, which is not observed. Nor can the agreement be the product of a low benchmark offsetting understated growth: the 1990 level is, if anything, 3 per cent above the independent 2011-based estimate (Table~\ref{tab:overlaps}, panel b), which would push the backcast levels up, not down. Broadberry and Burhop regarded time-series projections as consistent with direct benchmarks when they agree within 10 per cent, and judged a thirty-year projection that landed within 4 per cent a success;\footnote{\autocite[323--6]{broad2007}; \autocite[933]{broad2008}.} the Estonian overlap meets that standard over a longer span in each of four consecutive years.

The second overlap is less tidy, and instructively so. In 1990 the Soviet-period series stands 3.0 per cent above the modern series and in 1991 less than 1 per cent below it; thereafter the gap widens to \ovd{1992} per cent in 1992 and \ovd{1995} per cent by 1995. The agreement in 1990--1 validates the 1990 benchmark, and in particular the conversion from roubles to dollars: Bolotin's figure rests on purchasing-power parities, not exchange rates, and the modern series derives from an entirely independent ICP round, that of 2011, carried backwards with the World Bank's constant-price growth rates, so that two routes that share no data arrive at the same level within 3 per cent. The divergence after 1991 arises because the Soviet-period series continues to value the collapse of Soviet-era output at 1990 weights and, in trade, relies on deflated turnover figures that understate inflation: it records what happened to the Soviet-era economy, not what replaced it. The modern series is therefore preferred from 1991, the year in which the two agree. It is anchored on the MPD's 2011 figure for Estonia, 21,997~\intltwenty{} or 13,800~\intlnineteen{}, and carried back to 1990 with the World Bank's constant-price growth of GDP per head rather than with the MPD's own growth, because the two sources' Estonian growth rates converge only in 2011: the MPD's path implies a rise of 28 per cent between 1990 and 2011 where the national accounts record 69 per cent, so that its 1990 level, 17,247~\intltwenty{}, stands 32 per cent above the World Bank-based 13,033. Since the two share the 2011 anchor, the whole of the difference between the MPD and the new series at the end of the Soviet period lies in the growth attributed to 1990--2010, not in the level; and Bolotin's independent 1990 figure falls within 3 per cent of the World Bank-based one, which is the agreement that Table~\ref{tab:overlaps} reports.\footnote{\autocite{mpd2023}; \autocite{wdi2024}; the MPD's 1990 level is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:background}.} The MPD's 1989 figure lies 32 per cent above the new series (Table~\ref{tab:previous}), but its implied transition fall, 36 per cent against \eeTROUGHpct{} here, is similar: the two agree on the depth of the collapse though not on the level from which it started.\footnote{The comparison is developed in \autocite{klimantas2026soviet}.} The 1990-weighted and the 2011-based series bracket the true fall. The former values the collapse of the Soviet-era branches at their 1990 weights and misses the new activities, above all in services, that the IMF noted were emerging unrecorded; the latter, like Hamilton and de Vries's recent extrapolation of the post-Soviet republics from mid-1990s benchmarks, carries the weights of the surviving branches backwards and so records a shallower decline.\footnote{\autocite[11]{imf_transition}; \autocite[17]{hamilton_transition}; \autocite[22]{baltictransitioncontraction}.}

\subsection{A single late benchmark and the index-number problem}

The Soviet-period series extrapolates 1990 weights backwards over 1937--90, a period of profound structural change. An index with a late base year assigns to each sector the relative prices of the end of the period, and since the sectors that grew fastest were, as a rule, those whose relative prices fell, late weights give less importance to fast-growing sectors and so understate growth; conversely, early weights overstate it. Feinstein and Thomas put the point generally: an index with an early base `will always show a greater increase in quantities than one constructed with a base year late in the period'.\footnote{\autocite[516]{feinsteinthomas2002}.} This is the effect that Gerschenkron showed for Soviet machinery, and it was large in the Stalin period: Bergson's GNP for 1928--37 grows by 175 per cent at 1928 prices and by 62 per cent at 1937 prices.\footnote{\autocite[453]{gerschenkron1957}; \autocite[517]{feinsteinthomas2002}.} Three considerations establish that it is far smaller for the series presented here.

First, the effect is concentrated in the early phases of industrialisation, when relative prices change fastest; Jonas and Sardy showed that `most of the action which leads to the Gerschenkron effect takes place early'.\footnote{\autocite[85]{jonassardy1970}.} For the post-war Soviet economy the measured effect is modest. When the CIA moved its base year from 1970 to 1982 prices, its estimate of Soviet GNP growth over 1951--84 fell by about half a percentage point a year, from 5.6 to 5.2 per cent for the 1950s and from 3.2 to 2.4 per cent for the 1970s; an earlier comparison of 1960, 1970 and 1976 prices produced differences of a few tenths of a point, which the CIA judged `not large'.\footnote{\autocite[8]{cia1990}; \autocite[23--4]{cia1982a}; \autocite[156]{harrison1993}.} Half a point a year over forty years compounds to roughly a fifth of the level; this indicates the order of magnitude to be expected for the post-war decades rather than a bound, since the Estonian extrapolation also spans the forced industrialisation of the 1940s and 1950s, in which the effect is largest. Second, the bound must therefore come from the data, and the series can be tested. If 1990 weights understated Estonian growth over 1940--90, the Soviet-period series would arrive in 1937--40 systematically above the interwar series, which is weighted by the prices of 1938. It does not: the four differences are small and of both signs. The structural change within industry, where the Gerschenkron effect originates, is moreover handled at a finer level than the aggregate weights suggest, since the 101 product series are aggregated within eleven branches with 1986 weights before the branches are combined. Third, the procedure is that of every comparable reconstruction of Soviet output: the CIA used 1970 weights for 1950--80 and 1982 weights for 1950--90, and Maddison found `nothing untoward' in it; Kuboniwa's and Ponomarenko's reconstructions of Russian GDP for 1961--90 use, among their variants, the fixed structure of Russia's 1990 GDP.\footnote{\autocite[312]{Maddison1998b}; \autocite[57, 62]{ponomarenko2002}.} Fourth, the result can be set beside series built on other weights. For 1950--68, the phase in which a late base would depress measured growth most, the new series gives \gr{1950}{1968} per cent a year for Estonia, the same as the \grx{ECE}{1950}{1968} per cent of the East-Central European series that rest on Alton's multiple benchmarks and above the \grx{USSR}{1950}{1968} per cent of the CIA's 1982-weighted series for the Union (Table~\ref{tab:growth}); a series materially depressed by its weights would sit at the bottom of the bloc, and this one does not. A chain-linked alternative would require annual current-price value-added by sector, which exists for no Soviet republic, and a 1938-weighted alternative would require the sectors of 1990 to be valued at 1938 prices, when several of them did not exist. Nor has a benchmark at factor cost ever been constructed for a Soviet republic: the single-year study that Alton's project produced for Hungary runs to 254 pages, and a comparable exercise for a republic without price statistics of its own would be a monograph in itself.\footnote{\autocite{alton_hungary}.}

\subsection{Fixed value-added weights}

A related assumption is that the ratio of value-added to gross output within each sector remained at its benchmark-year level. The volume indices track gross output, inputs or employment and are combined with value-added weights, the method often loosely called single deflation.\footnote{\autocite[443--4]{thomasfeinstein2003}.} Thomas and Feinstein's evaluation of this method found that it understated true value-added growth by between 0.15 and 1 percentage point a year in their simulations, depending on the pace of technical change, and they described it as `the next best procedure' to double deflation where intermediate inputs cannot be observed.\footnote{\autocite[427--8, 444]{thomasfeinstein2003}.} Double deflation is not available for either period: the interwar statistical office recorded no intermediate consumption, and for the Soviet period, as Ponomarenko notes, the necessary price statistics `practically did not exist'; the CIA itself double-deflated only agriculture and used gross output as the index of value-added everywhere else.\footnote{\autocite[18--19]{ponomarenko2002}; \autocite[45]{cia1982a}.}

The bias is bounded with the Swedish historical national accounts, which permit single- and double-deflated series to be compared over comparable phases of industrialisation.\footnote{\autocite[19]{swedengdp_15602010}.} In the Swedish case single-deflated series understated the GDP deflator by 4 per cent over the 75 pre-industrial years and by 17 per cent over the 75 years after 1875, and the Swedish evidence also shows that single deflation distorts short-run movements more than long-run ones, since the outputs of one sector are the inputs of another and the errors partly offset in the aggregate.\footnote{\autocite[148--53]{lobellschonkrantz2008}.} For the interwar period, when input costs outside agriculture moved little, the maximum error is about 2 per cent of the level of GDP. For the Soviet period, where the sustained shift towards intermediate-intensive heavy industry makes changing ratios likely, it is put at 14 per cent over the half-century (online appendix), with the sign of Thomas and Feinstein's simulations, an understatement of growth and hence an overstatement of the backcast early levels; 14 per cent over fifty years is about a quarter of a point a year, in the lower part of their range, as befits indices that track gross output product by product rather than by broad branch. The Soviet economy, moreover, adhered to its mass-production methods throughout its existence, so that input ratios are likely to have been more stable than in industrialising Sweden.\footnote{\autocite[195--7]{berend_centraleastern19441993}.} Two observations limit the practical concern. The error accumulates over the extrapolation and is largest in the earliest years of the Soviet-period series, precisely where the overlap shows the series within 4 per cent of an independent level. And the opposite sign cannot be excluded, since a rising degree of fabrication would raise value-added ratios if it outpaced the relative fall in intermediate-input prices, which is why the overlap, not the simulations, is decisive; Gerschenkron, confronting constant ratios over half a century in Jostock's German accounts, thought them `not so very implausible' for just this reason.\footnote{\autocite[453]{gerschenkron1957}.}

\input{../../Tables/estonia/T7_sensitivity.tex}

Several deserve comment. The service sector of the 1938 benchmark, about one-third of GDP, rests on earnings and employment data and on Latvian structural ratios; an error of 10 per cent in the service estimate moves GDP by 3 per cent, and Norkus and Markevi\v{c}i\={u}t\.{e}'s independent approximation, 5 per cent below the new benchmark, suggests that any error is of that order. In the 1990 benchmark the allocation of value-added among services depends on Bolotin's Union-wide parities and the 1987 input--output table; a reallocation of a quarter of service value-added between branches moves GDP by about 4 per cent. The Soviet-period trade index uses turnover at comparable prices, the one value series in the reconstruction; Kontorovich's estimates of hidden inflation in Soviet retail trade imply an upward bias of at most 2 per cent of GDP.\footnote{\autocite[688--9]{kontorovich_2001}.} Defence after 1940 is unobserved, since the Soviet forces in Estonia were an all-Union institution of unknown size; their component of public services is interpolated between the 1940 and 1990 totals in step with civil employment, with a maximum bias of 5 per cent of GDP in either direction. The employment-based indices for health, education and administration make no allowance for productivity change, which understates service growth if productivity rose; the maximum effect is 10 per cent of the level. Finally, unregistered activity, perhaps 10--20 per cent of Soviet GDP in 1989, was of a size that OECD economies also displayed, so that its omission does not systematically impair international comparison.\footnote{\autocite[532--51]{kim2003}; \autocite[5]{baltictransitioncontraction}; \autocite[90--1]{shadow_europe}.}

Taken together, the quantifiable biases imply a margin of about 6 per cent around the interwar levels and, around the Soviet-period levels, of 14 per cent for the largest single bias, or nearly a quarter in the earliest years if the two same-signed biases of Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}, fixed value-added weights and unadjusted service productivity, were both at their maxima; the overlap shows that the realised error is far smaller. The war years are subject to a wider margin. The sub-period growth rates on which the argument of the following sections rests are robust to all of them: no plausible combination of the biases listed in Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} would raise growth in 1968--89 to the level of 1950--68, close the gap with Finland in 1938, or reverse Estonia's position relative to the Soviet average.
