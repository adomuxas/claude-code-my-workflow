\section{Estonian growth in comparative perspective}
\label{sec:implications}

The series spans three regimes, the capitalist republic of 1919--40, the planned economy of 1940--91 and the market economy restored after 1991, and the literature on catch-up growth asks the same questions of each. Gerschenkron's late industrialiser borrows technology rather than inventing it and so grows faster than the leader, as Solow's model predicts from diminishing returns to capital;\footnote{\autocite[8]{gerschenkron1962}; \autocite[70--1]{solow1956}.} but Abramovitz and David showed that the potential is realised only where the follower has the `social capability', the literacy, institutions and commercial organisation, to absorb what it borrows, and where the leader's technology is congruent with its endowments,\footnote{\autocite{abramovitz_david1996}.} and North, and Acemoglu and Robinson, make convergence conditional on institutions that reward productive investment.\footnote{\autocite{north1990}; \autocite{acemoglu_whynationsfail}.} Estonia is an unusually clean test, because its social capability was high throughout by the standards of the European periphery, while its institutions were changed twice from outside. Tables~\ref{tab:relative}, \ref{tab:interwar}, \ref{tab:trade} and~\ref{tab:structure} provide the comparative evidence.

\subsection{Interwar Estonia: recovery, depression and economic nationalism}

The interwar record falls into two phases. The 1920s were a recovery: income per head grew by \gr{1919}{1929} per cent a year but regained its 1913 level only in 1929. The sectors that led, industry, construction and transport, were those most damaged, and the limits of the recovery were set by the permanent loss of the imperial market: pre-war industry had been built to serve Russia, and attempts to recover a Russian transit trade through Tallinn came to little.\footnote{\autocite[193--210]{koll1994gap}.} Finland, less dependent on Russia and with forest industries that found Western buyers at once, grew by \grx{Finland}{1919}{1929} per cent a year over the same decade (Table~\ref{tab:growth}). Estonia's social capability was not in doubt, but the technology it had absorbed before 1914 was no longer congruent with the markets available to it.

The 1930s look different. The Depression was shallow, 8 per cent from peak to trough, and short; the recovery after 1933 was rapid, more than 4 per cent a year to 1938: Estonian income per head grew by \gr{1929}{1938} per cent a year between 1929 and 1938, when the Western core managed \grx{Western}{1929}{1938} per cent and East-Central Europe \grx{ECE}{1929}{1938} per cent (Table~\ref{tab:growth}). The timing is consistent with the exchange-rate explanation: Estonia clung to gold through the banking crisis of 1931--2 and recovered only after devaluing and pegging to sterling in June 1933, the pattern that Eichengreen and Sachs identified for Europe and that the Nordic economies displayed.\footnote{\autocite[74]{bernankejames01}; \autocite{RefWorks:doc:5ea847d2e4b0aa025739fe41}; \autocite{grytten_nordicdepression}; \autocite[287--99]{kuokstis2016}.} The content of the recovery was the policy that Kõll and Valge have called economic nationalism: state investment in oil shale, textiles and food processing, protection of the home market and the organisation of agricultural exports through co-operatives in imitation of Denmark.\footnote{\autocite[11--29]{koll1998}; \autocite{norkus_denmarkisation}; \autocite[118--35]{klesment2005}.} The sectoral indices confirm that the acceleration was industrial and urban (Table~\ref{tab:sectoral}).

Table~\ref{tab:interwar} places the result in its regional setting. In 1938 Estonia's income per head, \ee{1938}~\intlnineteen{}, was above that of Lithuania, Poland and Hungary, slightly below Czechoslovakia's and well below Latvia's, Finland's and the Nordic average. Its growth over 1929--38 was exceeded in the region only by its Baltic neighbours, Finland and the Soviet Union (whose figures for the period are contested), and was far above that of the Western core and East-Central Europe. Table~\ref{tab:structure} shows why the result should be read as a success and why it was not a greater one. Estonia had the ingredients of social capability: adult literacy of around 92 per cent, higher than in any country of eastern or southern Europe except Latvia and Czechoslovakia and above Finland's, university enrolment per head among the highest in Europe, and land in the hands of smallholders after the reform of 1919. Yet 63 per cent of the labour force still worked in agriculture in 1934, a share as high as Finland's, though below Latvia's, and agriculture produced some 38 per cent of GDP in 1937 (Table~\ref{tab:structure}). An economy of that structure, open to trade (Table~\ref{tab:trade}) but exporting butter, bacon, timber and flax, could hardly grow faster than its agricultural productivity and the markets Britain and Germany allowed it; the industrial drive of 1934--9 was a response to that constraint, not an escape from it.

\subsection{Under Soviet rule: collectivisation, industrialisation and stagnation}

The Soviet period opens with a decade of destruction. Income per head fell by about a third between 1940 and 1944 on the present estimates, and the resident population by a fifth between 1939 and 1945, of which about six points reflect the transfer of the districts east of Narva and of Petseri to Russia and the rest war deaths, deportation and flight.\footnote{\autocite[265]{taagepera_coll01}; \autocite[12]{deportations_ee}.} The recovery of industry and construction after 1945 was rapid, as Soviet reconstruction everywhere was: both regained their 1938 level by 1948, and industry grew by around 20 and construction by around 14 per cent a year between 1945 and 1953 (Tables~\ref{tab:sectoral} and~\ref{tab:appsec1}). The war had destroyed people rather than plant: with a fifth of the population gone, productive capacity stood underused, and the first years of recovery, in which the investment ratio was still below the Soviet mean even on official data, rested on putting labour, increasingly immigrant labour directed from Russia, back to work on existing capital, the mechanism that Vonyó and Markevich identify across post-war East-Central Europe.\footnote{\autocite[108]{years_of_dependence}; \autocite[290]{vonyoinmorys2021}; \autocite[126--8]{baltichistorykasekamp2017}.} Agriculture tells a different story. Livestock output, halved by the war, was still 38 per cent below its 1938 level in 1950, the year after collectivisation; crop output recovered sooner, but the agricultural sector as a whole stood at 60--65 per cent of its 1938 level in 1945--6, fluctuated between 77 and 93 per cent of it in 1947--57, and exceeded it only in \sregain{Primarysectortotal}. The index shows a shallower trough than Mertelsmann's archival estimate, which puts output after collectivisation at perhaps 40 per cent of the 1939 level, but the two agree that the sector did not recover for a decade, and that the collective and state farms produced little more than a third of output in the early 1950s, the rest coming from private plots, which still supplied 28 per cent in 1960.\footnote{\autocite[156--8]{olaf_stalin}; \autocite[79--109]{feest2014}; \autocite[257]{klesment2009}; \autocite[160]{hansen1994}.} Nothing comparable to the Soviet famine of 1932--3 occurred, probably because the Baltic collectivisation of 1949 did not coincide with a procurement crisis and because, as Harrison has argued for Soviet agriculture generally, the system had by then learned to extract the surplus without destroying the herds.\footnote{\autocite[192--208]{harrison1996}.} The cost of war and collectivisation together was a lost decade and a permanent shift of effort towards animal husbandry, in which Estonia came to lead the Union in output per head, while crop output never again exceeded the level it reached in \speak{Crops}.\footnote{\autocite[234]{years_of_dependence}.}

Between 1950 and 1968 Estonia experienced what Gerschenkron would have recognised as a classic burst of state-led industrialisation. Total GDP grew by \tgr{1950}{1968} per cent a year and income per head by \gr{1950}{1968} per cent, faster than in Finland, the Nordic countries or the Western core, though not than the Western periphery, whose Iberian and Greek members were in the middle of their own industrialisation (Table~\ref{tab:growth}). The engine was investment directed from Moscow into three complexes. The first was the oil-shale basin of the north-east, which fed two of the largest thermal power stations in the Union and raised electricity output from 2 billion kilowatt-hours in 1960 to 12 billion in 1970, two-thirds of it consumed outside the republic. The second was cotton textiles, expanded in Estonia rather than in cotton-growing Uzbekistan because Estonia had the skilled labour and the mills; the third, machine-building and electronics for the Union market.\footnote{\autocite[239]{years_of_dependence}; \autocite[41--7]{ahderajasalu1992}; \autocite[248--58]{pihlamagi2013}.} The pace was already slackening: industrial output had grown by 15 per cent a year in 1948--55 and grew by 9 per cent a year in the decade that followed, and the sovnarkhoz reform of 1957--65, which briefly placed industrial management in Tallinn, was Khrushchev's response to that deceleration across the Union rather than its cause. Baltic historiography has judged the reform favourably, perhaps more for the autonomy it conferred than for its results; it saw official industrial output double in seven years (the physical index used here rises by 80 per cent over 1957--64), with 70 per cent of investment going to industry and the share of light industry in the total deliberately reduced, and it was superseded in 1965 by the Kosygin reform and the recentralisation of enterprises.\footnote{\autocite[248--52]{pihlamagi2013}; \autocite[67--70]{purre1965}; \autocite[189]{years_of_dependence}.} The labour came partly from a countryside that collectivisation had released and partly from immigration: Estonia's population grew by half between 1950 and 1989 while the Estonian share of it, close to 97 per cent at the end of the war, had fallen to three-quarters by the census of 1959 and to little more than 60 per cent by that of 1989.\footnote{\autocite[158--217]{case_estonia}.}

How did this growth compare with that of the Union into which Estonia had been absorbed? Table~\ref{tab:relative} gives the answer, and it is not the one that either Soviet or nationalist accounts have assumed. Estonia entered the Union in 1940 with income per head \pctussr{1940} per cent of the Soviet average; it stood at \pctussr{1968} per cent in 1968 and at \pctussr{1989} per cent in 1989. The gap neither closed nor widened appreciably over half a century: Estonia grew with the Union, somewhat faster in the 1950s and 1960s and somewhat slower thereafter, and remained one of its two or three richest republics throughout. The result matches Soviet-era estimates that placed Estonia at 137--145 per cent of the Union average in 1960--70 by net material product, and agrees in direction, if not in magnitude, with the CIA's finding that Estonian national income per head grew faster than the Union's in 1961--9, 6.3 against 5.5 per cent a year on NMP-based data that overstate growth.\footnote{\autocite[169, 174]{schroeder1973}; \autocite[64--71]{bs_livingstandards1976}; \autocite[8--10]{cia1972}; \autocite[35--8]{bs_livingstandards1988}.} Did Moscow buy the loyalty of its western borderlands with investment? Investment per head in Estonia was above the Union average in the 1960s, second only to Kazakhstan, but the republic's share of investment in its own national income was below the Union average. Van Selm and Dölle's reconstruction of inter-republican transfers finds Estonia's net balance close to zero from 1966 to 1978, becoming a net recipient of 4--6 per cent of national income only after 1979 and of 13 per cent in 1988.\footnote{\autocite[22--9]{cia1972}; \autocite[53]{adirim1988}; \autocite[136--40]{vanselmdolle1993}.} For the years after 1966 for which transfers can be measured, Estonia's high income was not the product of net transfers; and over the period as a whole it was inherited rather than created, since the republic was richer than the Union at annexation and remained so in roughly the same proportion.

The deceleration after 1968 was general and, by the late 1970s, complete. Income per head grew by \gr{1968}{1989} per cent a year over 1968--89 and by \gr{1973}{1989} per cent after 1973, and the sectoral peaks cluster in a single decade: food processing in \speak{Food}, construction in \speak{Construction}, electricity and oil-shale output in 1979--80, heavy industry in \speak{Heavyindustries}.\footnote{Electricity peaked in 1979 and fell by 9 per cent to 1985; oil-shale mining levelled off after the arrival of natural gas (\autocite[239]{years_of_dependence}; \autocite{taagepera2018}).} The turning point coincided with the Prague Spring and the end of Moscow's tolerance of regional economic autonomy, and with the year in which the Union as a whole ceased to converge on Western levels, ahead of most other European communist economies.\footnote{\autocite[203, 227--8]{years_of_dependence}.} The sources point to three causes. The Union-wide investment slowdown imposed by the tenth and eleventh five-year plans fell on a republic whose growth had been investment-led and whose labour reserves were exhausted: further expansion required immigration or capital deepening, and the planners, whose investment effort shifted eastwards to Siberia in the 1970s, increasingly withheld both; on official data the investment ratio in Estonia fell below the Soviet average by the mid-1970s and stayed there.\footnote{\autocite[128--63]{hanson2003}; \autocite[164--76]{kolde1978}; \autocite[441]{dienes1972}; \autocite[2]{ciasovietrep1970}.} The oil-shale complex, built on a resource that was expensive to mine and polluting to burn, lost its rationale once Siberian gas reached the Baltic.\footnote{\autocite[188--9]{olaf_stalin}.} And the gains in reported output after 1975 were increasingly nominal: Finnish and Estonian economists who compared the two economies in 1992 concluded that the rapid growth of Estonian industry to 1975 reflected real investment, but that `the further growth of production can be explained mainly by the change in prices'.\footnote{\autocite[39--40]{ahderajasalu1992}.} The physical indices used here confirm that judgement: industrial output grew by \sgr{Industrytotal}{1968}{1989} per cent a year after 1968, and the branches that continued to grow, textiles and other light industry, did so slowly, while cotton cloth output was flat from 1970.\footnote{\autocite[41]{ahderajasalu1992}.} The textile index itself fell by 14 per cent between 1972 and 1975 and did not regain its 1972 level until 1985 (Table~\ref{tab:appsec1}), the one sharp break in a branch that otherwise grew throughout the Soviet period; the sources consulted offer no explanation, and the authors record it rather than interpret it. Three further irregularities in the indices deserve mention. Construction, which had grown by about 9 per cent a year since 1950, peaked in \speak{Construction} and stagnated for the rest of the Soviet period as the Union-wide investment squeeze took hold; crop output fell by a quarter in each of the poor harvests of 1978 and 1981 that affected the Union as a whole; and the housing index, which is derived from construction activity and population and had grown by between 2 and 3 per cent a year since the 1950s, stopped growing in 1986, when completions began to fall from 13,500 dwellings a year in 1985 to 5,200 in 1991; its slight decline thereafter reflects the construction of the proxy rather than a physical loss of stock.\footnote{\autocite[134--50]{hanson2003}; \autocite[60, 66]{ahderajasalu1992}; \autocite[216]{wb1993estonia}.}

Read through the lens of growth theory, the Soviet episode is coherent. The catch-up of 1950--68 is what the models predict when a capital-poor economy with high social capability is made to invest, and mid-century mass-production methods were congruent with Estonia's literate and urbanised labour force; the stagnation that followed is equally what they predict when the institutional condition fails. Extensive growth exhausted its inputs, and the system could not replace them with the productivity growth that flexible production, foreign technology and competition were delivering across the Gulf: the share of services in Estonian GDP was 42 per cent in 1990, barely above its interwar level, against about 60 per cent in Finland.\footnote{Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}; \autocite[11--12]{ahderajasalu1992}; \autocite{klimantas2026soviet}.} Estonia's income relative to the Western core, which had risen from \pctwc{1950} per cent in 1950 to \pctwc{1968} per cent in 1968, fell back to \pctwc{1989} per cent by 1989, almost exactly its level of 1913. Two decades of Soviet growth had been cancelled by two decades of Soviet stagnation; by 1989 Greece, the poorest capitalist economy in Western Europe, had overtaken Estonia, one of the richest parts of the socialist world after Slovenia (Table~\ref{tab:relative}).

\subsection{Transition and recovery}

The transition compressed into four years a fall in income per head of \eeTROUGHpct{} per cent, deeper than the Depression and comparable only to the two world wars. The sectoral indices to 1991 show the collapse beginning in Soviet-era industry and construction, which were already shrinking at \sgr{Industrytotal}{1989}{1991} and \sgr{Construction}{1989}{1991} per cent a year before the rouble was abandoned. After 1991 the modern series records the loss of eastern markets and cheap Soviet inputs, the currency board of 1992 and the stabilisation that followed.\footnote{\autocite[1--8]{wb1993estonia}; \autocite[164--6]{norkus_restorations}; \autocite[482--509]{shteinbuka1993}.} By comparison with the other Soviet republics the fall was contained, probably because Estonia had less heavy industry to lose, 41 per cent of industrial value-added in 1990 against 48 in Latvia and 55 in Lithuania, and reoriented trade towards Finland and Sweden faster than any other successor state.\footnote{\autocite[184--6]{bolotin1992}; \autocite[22]{transition_greenes}; \autocite[117]{baltistates_smith_estonia}; \autocite[443--4]{transition_progress}.} Recovery from 1995 at \gr{1995}{2022} per cent a year carried Estonia from \pctwc{1995} per cent of the Western core in 1995 to \pctwc{2022} per cent in 2022, close to the \pctwc{1968} per cent reached in 1968 at the height of Soviet growth. Whether the market-era catch-up will continue or stall in the middle-income trap that Staehr describes is a question for another paper; the long series shows that Estonia converged on Western Europe under capitalism twice, in the 1930s and after 1995, and under planning once, briefly, before diverging again.\footnote{\autocite[274--80]{staehr2015}.}
