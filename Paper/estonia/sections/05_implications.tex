\section{Estonian growth in comparative perspective}
\label{sec:implications}

The series spans three regimes, the capitalist republic of 1919--40, the planned economy of 1940--91 and the market economy restored after 1991, and the literature on catch-up growth asks the same questions of each. Gerschenkron's late industrialiser borrows technology rather than inventing it and so grows faster than the leader, as Solow's model predicts from diminishing returns to capital;\footnote{\autocite[8]{gerschenkron1962}; \autocite[70--1]{solow1956}.} but Abramovitz and David showed that the potential is realised only where the follower has the `social capability', the literacy, institutions and commercial organisation, to absorb what it borrows, and where the leader's technology is congruent with its endowments,\footnote{\autocite{abramovitz_david1996}.} and North, and Acemoglu and Robinson, make convergence conditional on institutions that reward productive investment.\footnote{\autocite{north1990}; \autocite{acemoglu_whynationsfail}.} Estonia is an unusually clean test, because its social capability was high throughout by the standards of the European periphery, while its institutions were changed twice from outside. Tables~\ref{tab:relative}, \ref{tab:interwar}, \ref{tab:trade} and~\ref{tab:structure} provide the comparative evidence.

\subsection{Interwar Estonia: recovery, depression and economic nationalism}

The interwar record falls into two phases. The 1920s were a recovery: income per head grew by \gr{1919}{1929} per cent a year but regained its 1913 level only in 1929. The sectors that led, industry, construction and transport, were those most damaged, and the limits of the recovery were set in the world market that replaced the imperial one: pre-war industry had been built to serve Russia, and Kõll argues that its slow recovery after that market was lost owed as much to the competitiveness of Estonian producers and to demand in their new Western markets as to the loss itself.\footnote{\autocite[199--200]{koll1994gap}.} Finland, less dependent on Russia and with forest industries that found Western buyers at once, grew by \grx{Finland}{1919}{1929} per cent a year over the same decade (Table~\ref{tab:growth}). Estonia's social capability was not in doubt, but the technology it had absorbed before 1914 was no longer congruent with the markets available to it.

The 1930s look different. The Depression was shallow, 8 per cent from peak to trough, and short; the recovery after 1933 was rapid, more than 4 per cent a year to 1938: Estonian income per head grew by \gr{1929}{1938} per cent a year between 1929 and 1938, when the Western core managed \grx{Western}{1929}{1938} per cent and East-Central Europe \grx{ECE}{1929}{1938} per cent (Table~\ref{tab:growth}). The timing is consistent with the exchange-rate explanation: Estonia clung to gold through the banking crisis of 1931--2, and the recovery, of which there were signs from 1932, gathered momentum only after Estonia devalued and pegged the kroon to sterling in June 1933, the pattern that Eichengreen and Sachs identified for Europe and that the Nordic economies displayed.\footnote{\autocite[74, 92, 100]{bernankejames01}; \autocite[41]{koll1998policy}; \autocite{RefWorks:doc:5ea847d2e4b0aa025739fe41}; \autocite{grytten_nordicdepression}.} The content of the recovery was the policy that Kõll and Valge have called economic nationalism: priority for the state oil-shale industry, protection of the home market, behind which non-priority branches such as textiles also grew fast, and the organisation of agricultural exports through co-operatives in imitation of Denmark.\footnote{\autocite[13, 21]{koll1998}; \autocite[171--5]{koll1998results}; \autocite[36]{koll1998policy}; \autocite{norkus_denmarkisation}; \autocite[118--35]{klesment2005}.} The sectoral indices confirm that the acceleration was industrial and urban (Table~\ref{tab:sectoral}).

Table~\ref{tab:interwar} places the result in its regional setting. In 1938 Estonia's income per head, \ee{1938}~\intlnineteen{}, was above that of Lithuania, Poland and Hungary, slightly below Czechoslovakia's and well below Latvia's, Finland's and the Nordic average. Its growth over 1929--38 was exceeded in the region only by its Baltic neighbours, Finland and the Soviet Union (whose figures for the period are contested), and was far above that of the Western core and East-Central Europe. Table~\ref{tab:structure} shows why the result should be read as a success and why it was not a greater one. Estonia had the ingredients of social capability: adult literacy of around 92 per cent, higher than in any country of eastern or southern Europe except Latvia and Czechoslovakia and above Finland's, university enrolment per head among the highest in Europe, and land in the hands of smallholders after the reform of 1919. Yet 63 per cent of the labour force still worked in agriculture in 1934, a share as high as Finland's, though below Latvia's, and agriculture produced some 38 per cent of GDP in 1937 (Table~\ref{tab:structure}). An economy of that structure, open to trade (Table~\ref{tab:trade}) but exporting butter, bacon, timber and flax, could hardly grow faster than its agricultural productivity and the markets Britain and Germany allowed it; the industrial drive of 1934--9 was a response to that constraint, not an escape from it.

\subsection{Under Soviet rule: collectivisation, industrialisation and stagnation}

The Soviet period opens with a decade of destruction. On the present estimates, income per head fell by about a third between 1940 and 1944, and the resident population by a fifth between 1939 and 1945 (Table~\ref{tab:series}), of which about six points reflect the transfer of the districts east of Narva and most of Petseri county to Russia and the rest war deaths, deportation and flight.\footnote{\autocite[74--5]{years_of_dependence}, who put the ceded area at 6 per cent of Estonia's pre-war population.} The recovery of industry and construction after 1945 was rapid, as Soviet reconstruction everywhere was: both regained their 1938 level by 1948, and industry grew by around 20 and construction by around 14 per cent a year between 1945 and 1953 (Tables~\ref{tab:sectoral} and~\ref{tab:appsec1}). The war had destroyed people rather than plant: with some 15 per cent of the population lost, productive capacity stood underused, and the first years of recovery rested on putting labour, increasingly immigrant labour directed from Russia, back to work on existing capital, the mechanism that Vonyó and Markevich identify across post-war East-Central Europe.\footnote{\autocite[108]{years_of_dependence}; \autocite[290]{vonyoinmorys2021}; \autocite[154--5]{kasekamp2010}.} Agriculture tells a different story. Livestock output, halved by the war, was still 38 per cent below its 1938 level in 1950, the year after collectivisation; crop output recovered sooner, but the agricultural sector as a whole stood at 60--65 per cent of its 1938 level in 1945--6, fluctuated between 77 and 93 per cent of it in 1947--57, and exceeded it only in \sregain{Primarysectortotal}. The index shows a shallower trough than Mertelsmann's archival estimate, which puts output after collectivisation at perhaps 40 per cent of the 1939 level, but the two agree that the sector did not recover for a decade, and that the collective and state farms produced little more than a third of output in the mid-1950s, the rest coming from private plots, which still supplied 28 per cent in 1960.\footnote{\autocite[156--8]{olaf_stalin}; \autocite[257]{klesment2009}; \autocite[160]{hansen1994}.} Nothing comparable to the Soviet famine of 1932--3 occurred, probably because the Baltic collectivisation of 1949 did not coincide with a procurement crisis and because, as Harrison notes for Soviet agriculture generally, the state had learned through the collectivisation of the 1930s how to control the peasantry.\footnote{\autocite[192--208]{harrison1996}.} The cost of war and collectivisation together was a lost decade and a permanent shift of effort towards animal husbandry, in which Estonia came to lead the Union in butter output per head, while crop output never again exceeded the level it reached in \speak{Crops}.\footnote{\autocite[234]{years_of_dependence}.}

Between 1950 and 1968 Estonia experienced what Gerschenkron would have recognised as a classic burst of state-led industrialisation. Total GDP grew by \tgr{1950}{1968} per cent a year and income per head by \gr{1950}{1968} per cent, faster than in Finland, the Nordic countries or the Western core, though not than the Western periphery, whose Iberian and Greek members were in the middle of their own industrialisation (Table~\ref{tab:growth}). The engine was investment directed from Moscow into three complexes. The first was the oil-shale basin of the north-east, which fed the world's two largest oil-shale power stations and raised electricity output from 2 billion kilowatt-hours in 1960 to nearly 12 billion in 1970, roughly two-thirds of which, on Misiunas and Taagepera's figures, was consumed outside the republic. The second was cotton textiles, expanded in Estonia, which was short of labour, rather than in cotton-growing Uzbekistan, which was not, a choice that kept both republics dependent on Moscow; the third, machine-building and electronics for the Union market.\footnote{\autocite[239]{years_of_dependence}, for the share consumed outside the republic; \autocite[41--7]{ahderajasalu1992}, table 12 for the output figures; \autocite[248--58]{pihlamagi2013}.} The pace was already slackening: industrial output had grown by 15 per cent a year in 1948--55 and grew by 9 per cent a year in the decade that followed, and the sovnarkhoz reform of 1957--65, which briefly placed industrial management in Tallinn, was Khrushchev's response to that deceleration across the Union rather than its cause. Baltic historiography has judged the reform favourably, perhaps more for the autonomy it conferred than for its results; it saw official industrial output double in seven years (the physical index used here rises by 80 per cent over 1957--64), with 70 per cent of investment going to industry and the share of light industry in the total deliberately reduced, and it was superseded in 1965 by the Kosygin reform and the recentralisation of enterprises.\footnote{\autocite[248--52]{pihlamagi2013}; \autocite[67--70]{purre1965}; \autocite[188]{years_of_dependence}.} The labour came partly from a countryside that collectivisation had released and partly from immigration: Estonia's population grew by half between 1950 and 1989 (Table~\ref{tab:series}) while the Estonian share of it, over 90 per cent at the end of the war, had fallen to three-quarters by the census of 1959 and to 62 per cent by that of 1989.\footnote{\autocite[35--41, 111]{case_estonia}; \autocite[154]{kasekamp2010}.}

The expansion was interrupted once. In 1962 the crop index fell by 19 per cent and the primary sector by 9 per cent; total GDP grew by 2 per cent and income per head by less than 1 per cent, against about 5 per cent a year in the surrounding years (Tables~\ref{tab:series} and~\ref{tab:appsec1}). The check was the Estonian instance of the agricultural crisis that overtook the whole Union in 1962--3. Recent Russian studies trace that crisis to the restrictions placed on private plots and private livestock from 1958, which moved animals into collective herds that had no fodder for them, to the exhaustion of the virgin-lands and maize campaigns, and finally to the drought of 1963 in Kazakhstan and Siberia, which cut the Union grain harvest from 83 million tons in 1962 to 63 million. The leadership raised state purchase prices for livestock by 35 per cent and the retail prices of meat by 30 and of butter by 25 per cent from 1 June 1962, suppressed the strike that followed at Novocherkassk, and in the autumn of 1963 rationed bread in many cities and bought grain abroad on a scale without precedent.\footnote{\autocite[17--19]{belkharoev2024}; \autocite[111--13]{andreenkov2021}.} Estonia's harvest failed a year before the Union's. Its grain deliveries to the all-Union fund fell from 30,000 tons in 1961 to 13,000 in 1962, collective farms were gathering fir needles for fodder in the winter of 1962--3, and the herd, 307,000 cows at the start of 1963, was far short of the 432,000 that the seven-year plan had set for 1965.\footnote{\autocite[34--5, 55]{purre1964}, a pamphlet compiled from the republican press.} Crops recovered in 1963, when Estonian grain deliveries to the all-Union fund rose to more than twice their 1961 level as the rest of the Union went short, but livestock output stood just below its 1938 level from 1961 to 1964, and the primary sector as a whole was back at its 1961 level only in 1963 and above it in 1964. Whether the Estonian failure of 1962 owed more to the season or to the sowing plans and fodder policy that Purre documented cannot be settled from the index. What the index shows is that the crisis cost Estonia a year of growth rather than, as in much of the Union, a winter of bread queues: industry grew by 7 per cent in 1962, little below its pace in the years around it, and the loss was confined to the farms.

How did this growth compare with that of the Union into which Estonia had been absorbed? Table~\ref{tab:relative} gives the answer, and it is not the one that either Soviet or nationalist accounts have assumed. Estonia entered the Union in 1940 with income per head \pctussr{1940} per cent of the Soviet average; it stood at \pctussr{1968} per cent in 1968 and at \pctussr{1989} per cent in 1989. The gap neither closed nor widened appreciably over half a century: Estonia grew with the Union, somewhat faster in the 1950s and 1960s and somewhat slower thereafter, and remained one of its two or three richest republics throughout. The result matches Soviet-era estimates that placed Estonia at 137--145 per cent of the Union average in 1960--70 by net material product, and agrees in direction, if not in magnitude, with the CIA's finding that Estonian national income per head grew faster than the Union's in 1961--9, 6.3 against 5.5 per cent a year on NMP-based data that overstate growth.\footnote{Net material product: \autocite[169, 174]{schroeder1973}; CIA: \autocite[8--10]{cia1972}. Koropeckyj's GNP-based estimate puts Estonia at 134 per cent of the Union average in 1970, and Bohnet and Penkaitis find Estonian national income per head some 15 per cent above the RSFSR's in 1970--84: \autocite[63--4]{bs_livingstandards1976}; \autocite[35--6]{bs_livingstandards1988}.} Did Moscow buy the loyalty of its western borderlands with investment? Investment per head in Estonia was above the Union average in the 1960s, second only to Kazakhstan, but the share of accumulation in the national income it used was below the Union average from 1965, the first year of Adirim's comparison. Van Selm and Dölle's reconstruction of inter-republican transfers finds Estonia's net balance close to zero from 1966 to 1978, becoming a net recipient of 4--6 per cent of national income only after 1979 and of 13 per cent in 1988.\footnote{Investment per head: \autocite[22--9]{cia1972}; accumulation share: \autocite[53]{adirim1988}; transfers: \autocite[136--40]{vanselmdolle1993}.} Estonia's high measured output was not the product of net transfers, which became significant only from 1979, when its relative position was long established; transfers affected what the republic consumed rather than what it produced. Over the period as a whole its high income was inherited rather than created, since the republic was richer than the Union at annexation and remained so in roughly the same proportion.

The deceleration after 1968 was general and, by the late 1970s, complete. Income per head grew by \gr{1968}{1989} per cent a year over 1968--89 and by \gr{1973}{1989} per cent after 1973, and the sectoral peaks cluster in a single decade: food processing in \speak{Food}, construction in \speak{Construction}, electricity and oil-shale output in 1979--80, heavy industry in \speak{Heavyindustries}.\footnote{On electricity, which peaked in 1979 and fell by 9 per cent to 1985, \autocite{taagepera2018}; on oil shale, \autocite[239]{years_of_dependence}.} The turning point coincided with the Prague Spring, a `psychological watershed' that marked, in Misiunas and Taagepera's words, `the end of a cooperative evolution of Moscow's rule in the Baltic republics', and followed the recentralisation of economic management after 1964.\footnote{\autocite[203, 227--8]{years_of_dependence}.} The sources point to three causes. The Union-wide investment slowdown imposed by the tenth and eleventh five-year plans fell on a republic whose growth had been investment-led and whose labour reserves were exhausted: further expansion required immigration or capital deepening, and the planners, whose investment effort shifted eastwards to Siberia from the late 1960s, increasingly withheld both. On official data the share of accumulation in the national income Estonia used was below the Union average in each of Adirim's benchmark years from 1965 to 1983, the gap widening from 4.6 points in 1965 to 6.7 in 1983, although the Union figure includes all-Union military outlays.\footnote{On the slowdown and the turn from labour supply to labour productivity, \autocite[128--63]{hanson2003}; \autocite[174]{kolde1978}; on the eastward shift of investment, \autocite[441, 446]{dienes1972}; \autocite[2]{ciasovietrep1970}; on the accumulation share, \autocite[53]{adirim1988}.} The oil-shale complex, built on a resource that was polluting to burn, stopped expanding once natural gas reached the Baltic cities by pipeline: mining levelled off in the 1970s, although Moscow sought to raise it again around 1980.\footnote{\autocite[188--9]{olaf_stalin}; \autocite[239]{years_of_dependence}.} And the gains in reported output after 1975 were increasingly nominal: Finnish and Estonian economists who compared the two economies in 1992 concluded that the rapid growth of Estonian industry to 1975 reflected real investment, but that `the further growth of production can be explained mainly by the change in prices'.\footnote{\autocite[39--40]{ahderajasalu1992}.} The physical indices used here confirm that judgement: industrial output grew by \sgr{Industrytotal}{1968}{1989} per cent a year after 1968, and the branches that continued to grow, textiles and other light industry, did so slowly, while cotton cloth output was flat from 1970.\footnote{\autocite[41]{ahderajasalu1992}.} The textile index itself fell by 14 per cent between 1972 and 1975 and did not regain its 1972 level until 1985 (Table~\ref{tab:appsec1}), the one sharp break in a branch that otherwise grew throughout the Soviet period; the sources consulted offer no explanation, and the authors record it rather than interpret it. Three further irregularities in the indices deserve mention. Construction, which had grown by about 9 per cent a year since 1950, peaked in \speak{Construction} and stagnated for the rest of the Soviet period as the Union-wide investment squeeze took hold; crop output fell by a quarter in each of the poor harvests of 1978 and 1981, the second of which affected the Union as a whole; and the housing index, which is derived from construction activity and population and had grown by between 2 and 3 per cent a year since the 1950s, stopped growing in 1986, although residential construction rose until 1987 and completions, about 13,500 dwellings a year from 1985, fell only from 1989, to 5,200 in 1991; its slight decline thereafter reflects the construction of the proxy rather than a physical loss of stock.\footnote{\autocite[134--50]{hanson2003}; \autocite[60, 62]{ahderajasalu1992}; on dwelling completions, \autocite[216]{wb1993estonia}.}

Read through the lens of growth theory, the Soviet episode is coherent. The catch-up of 1950--68 is what the models predict when a capital-poor economy with high social capability is made to invest, and mid-century mass-production methods were congruent with Estonia's literate and urbanised labour force; the stagnation that followed is equally what they predict when the institutional condition fails. Extensive growth exhausted its inputs, and the system could not replace them with the productivity growth that flexible production, foreign technology and competition were delivering across the Gulf: the share of services in Estonian GDP was 42 per cent in 1990, barely above its interwar level, against about 60 per cent in Finland.\footnote{For Estonia, Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}; for Finland, \autocite[11--12]{ahderajasalu1992}; \autocite[25]{klimantas2026soviet}. Ahde and Rajasalu, on official data, put the primary and secondary sectors at more than two-thirds of Estonian GDP in 1989.} Estonia's income relative to the Western core, which had risen from \pctwc{1950} per cent in 1950 to \pctwc{1968} per cent in 1968, fell back to \pctwc{1989} per cent by 1989, almost exactly its level of 1913. Two decades of Soviet growth had been cancelled by two decades of Soviet stagnation; by 1989 Greece, the poorest capitalist economy in Western Europe, had overtaken Estonia, one of the richest parts of the socialist world after Slovenia (Table~\ref{tab:relative}).

\subsection{Transition and recovery}

The transition compressed into four years a fall in income per head of \eeTROUGHpct{} per cent, deeper than the Depression and comparable only to the two world wars. The sectoral indices to 1991 show the collapse beginning in Soviet-era industry and construction, which were already shrinking at \sgr{Industrytotal}{1989}{1991} and \sgr{Construction}{1989}{1991} per cent a year before the rouble was abandoned. After 1991 the modern series records the loss of eastern markets and cheap Soviet inputs, the currency board of 1992 and the stabilisation that followed.\footnote{\autocite[2--7]{wb1993estonia}; \autocite[482--509]{shteinbuka1993}.} By comparison with the other Soviet republics the fall was contained, probably because Estonia had less heavy industry to lose, 41 per cent of industrial value-added in 1990 against 48 in Latvia and 53 in Lithuania, and quickly reoriented its trade towards Finland and Sweden.\footnote{Heavy industry comprises power, fuels, metals, chemicals, machine-building and building materials; the shares are calculated from Bolotin's table 5, with his dollar values converted back to roubles at his branch-specific dollar conversion ratios: \autocite[190]{bolotin1992}. On the milder crisis and the reorientation, \autocite[164]{norkus_restorations}; \autocite[22]{transition_greenes}; \autocite[117]{baltistates_smith_estonia}; \autocite[454]{transition_progress}.} Recovery from 1995 at \gr{1995}{2022} per cent a year carried Estonia from \pctwc{1995} per cent of the Western core in 1995 to \pctwc{2022} per cent in 2022, close to the \pctwc{1968} per cent reached in 1968 at the height of Soviet growth. Whether the market-era catch-up will continue or stall in the middle-income trap that Staehr describes is a question for another paper; the long series shows that Estonia converged on Western Europe under capitalism twice, in the 1930s and after 1995, and under planning once, briefly, before diverging again.\footnote{\autocite[274--80]{staehr2015}.}
