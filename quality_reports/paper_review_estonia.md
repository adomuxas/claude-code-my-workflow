## Summary Assessment

**Recommendation: Revise & Resubmit (major revision)**

The paper builds the first annual, internationally comparable GDP-per-capita series for Estonia over 1919–95 and links it to 1913 and 2022. It does this with a new 1938 Estonia–Sweden consumer-price PPP benchmark, a recalculated interwar production series, and a Soviet-period series of physical-output and employment indices weighted by Bolotin's 1990 sector structure. The contribution is real and well suited to EcHR, EEH or Cliometrica. Non-Russian Soviet republics have almost no modern national accounts. The series is ready for the Maddison Project. The archival work behind it (fond R-10, R-1969; 101 product series) is substantial. The prose is unusually good for the genre, and the paper handles the CIA/Bergson/Khanin literature carefully. The number macros are generated by R. I spot-checked more than 80 figures quoted in the text against the tables, and almost all agree. That is a credit to the pipeline.

The paper's validation logic is weaker than the text says, though, and the headline Finland result rests on choices that are not stress-tested. The 1937–40 overlap is the paper's main defence against every index-number objection. But the Soviet-period series reaches 1937–40 through a wartime link that the paper itself puts at ±15–25 per cent, partly borrowed from Lithuanian ratios. So the overlap tests a *net* error and cannot be used, as §IV does, to rule out specific biases one by one. Table 7 lists biases of up to −24 per cent on early Soviet levels. That is hard to square with an overlap within 4 per cent unless some offsetting error exists, and the war link is the obvious candidate. The 1950 level, which drives the "convergence in 1938–68" story, is not constrained by the overlap at all.

The headline claim is that "almost half of the 1989 gap with Finland had opened by 1938". It depends on (i) a single end year, 1938 (the ratio is 75 in 1938, 80 in 1939 and 86 in 1940); (ii) a 1938 level for Estonia from a direct PPP set against a 1938 level for Finland obtained by backcasting MPD's 1990 benchmark; and (iii) an interwar benchmark that the paper's own independent check (Norkus–Markevičiūtė) puts 11 per cent lower. None of this is bounded quantitatively. I also found some internal inconsistencies:

- The paper says 1962 was the only interruption of 1950–68 growth, but per-capita income fell in 1953.
- The explanation for the post-1991 divergence runs the wrong way.
- The paper's account of where the MPD's 1990 level comes from in §I conflicts with the account in §IV.
- Estonia relative to the USSR is measured against an MPD Union total about 10 per cent above Bolotin's own.

All of these can be fixed, and fixing them would make a strong paper considerably more convincing.

## Strengths

1. **A real gap filled.** This is an annual 1919–95 series in both Int$1990 and Int$2011, with population, sectoral indices (Tables A2–A3) and an online workbook. It is directly usable by the MPD and by comparative work on Soviet republics.
2. **Method follows the best practice for Soviet output.** It relies on physical quantities rather than value aggregates (02_method.tex:15–17). The positioning against Khanin, Harrison, Ponomarenko and Vonyó–Markevich is precise, and the paper is candid about the one value series it uses (trade turnover).
3. **Two independent validation points.** The 1937–40 and 1990–91 overlaps are reported transparently in Table 6 (T3_overlaps), with Feinstein grades and a clear sign convention.
4. **Internal arithmetic is mostly very good.** Growth rates, cumulative changes, peak and regain years, the 1938 benchmark arithmetic (services sum to 228.5; 592 kroon per head; 54.8 per cent of Sweden; 2,685 Int$1990) and the comparisons with earlier estimates (Table 1) all check out against `numbers.tex` and the appendix tables.
5. **Candour about weak spots.** The paper flags the wartime estimates as "rough", records the 1972–75 textile break rather than explaining it away, and describes the housing proxy's post-1986 behaviour as an artefact.
6. **Writing.** It reads clearly and confidently, with good EHR house style. The narrative of §V (sovnarkhoz, the 1962 crisis, the 1968 turning point, the transfers evidence) is well integrated with the indices.

## Major Concerns

### MC1. The 1937–40 overlap is used to rule out biases it cannot identify separately
- **Dimension:** Identification / methodology
- **Issue:** The text says the overlap "tests their combined size" (04_limitations.tex:34). It then uses the overlap to dismiss the Gerschenkron and late-weight bias specifically ("Second, the bound must therefore come from the data ... It does not", 04_limitations.tex:42) and to call fixed value-added weights a "practical" non-issue (04_limitations.tex:48). But the Soviet series reaches 1937–40 through the 1941–5 link, which is interpolated, partly taken from Lithuanian ratios, and graded 15–25 per cent (02_method.tex:51; Table 7 last row). Table 7 itself admits same-signed biases of −14 and −10 per cent on early levels (04_limitations.tex:58: "nearly a quarter"). For the overlap to hold while those biases are present, something must offset them, and the war link is the natural candidate. The overlap therefore validates the *sum* of extrapolation and wartime-link errors, not each one. The paper also says a 0.1 pp/yr understatement "would place the series 5 per cent above ... which is not observed". That holds only if the wartime link is exact.
- **Suggestion:**
  - Rewrite the claims so they cover net error only.
  - Report the overlap twice, once with the Lithuanian-ratio link and once with alternatives (for example, linear interpolation 1940–45, or ratios from Latvia or from German occupation data) to show how much the 1937–40 gap moves.
  - Bring Table 7's bounds into line with the overlap. Either explain what offsets the −24 per cent, or narrow the bounds.
- **Location:** 04_limitations.tex:34, 42, 48, 58; 02_method.tex:51; Table 7 (T7_sensitivity.tex:22–26)

### MC2. The headline Finland result (1938 ratio; "almost half the 1989 gap") is fragile and has no uncertainty band
- **Dimension:** Methodology / claims vs evidence
- **Issue:**
  - **(a) End year.** The Estonia/Finland ratio is 79 (1937), 75 (1938), 80 (1939) and 86 (1940) (numbers.tex). With 1939 as the "pre-occupation" year, the share of the 1989 log gap already open falls from 46 to about 35 per cent; with the 1934–39 average (about 77.5) it is about 40 per cent; with 1940 it is 24 per cent. The intro's phrase "before a Soviet soldier crossed the border" (main.tex:139) fits 1939 better than 1938, since the Soviet bases date from October 1939.
  - **(b) Mixed level sources.** Estonia's 1938 level comes from a direct Estonia–Sweden PPP, whereas Finland's 1938 level relative to Sweden (3,589/4,901 = 73 per cent, Table 9) is an MPD backcast from a modern benchmark. The headline ratio therefore mixes a direct and an indirect comparison. By the paper's own Gerschenkron argument, a fast-growing Finland backcast with late weights could have its 1938 level misstated.
  - **(c) Benchmark uncertainty.** Interwar levels carry "about 6 per cent" uncertainty (04_limitations.tex:58). Moving the 1938 level by ±6 per cent changes the log gap from 0.29 to roughly 0.23–0.35, so the share of the 1989 gap opened by 1938 ranges from about 37 to 55 per cent.
- **Suggestion:**
  - Give the 1938 ratio and the "share of gap" statistic with an explicit range: alternative end years (1937–39 averages), the ±6 per cent level band, and the Norkus–Markevičiūtė alternative.
  - Discuss, or better, carry out a direct Estonia–Finland 1938 price comparison, or cross-check Finland/Sweden 1938 against independent interwar PPP evidence such as Prados de la Escosura (2000).
  - Tone the abstract down to match ("between a third and a half").
- **Location:** main.tex:118, 139; 06_finland.tex:9, 11; 07_conclusion.tex:24; Table 8 (T8_relative.tex)

### MC3. The 1950 level and the 1938–68 "convergence" are where the backcast error is largest, and the overlap does not constrain them
- **Dimension:** Methodology / robustness
- **Issue:**
  - Several key findings rest on 1950s–60s levels backcast from 1990 over 40 years: 1938–50 growth of 2.6 per cent a year, called "striking" beside Lithuania and Finland (03_results.tex:15); the Finland ratio rising 75 → 86 → 91 ("the first half of Soviet rule was a period of convergence", 06_finland.tex:11); and the Western-core ratio of 66 → 70.
  - The 1950 level does not pass through the war link, so the 1937–40 overlap gives it no support.
  - With the paper's own fixed-weight bias of about 0.25 pp/yr, 1950 could be overstated by about 10 per cent. Add the service-productivity bias and it could be about 20 per cent. A 10–14 per cent overstatement in 1950 would remove the 1938–50 convergence with Finland entirely (86 → about 75).
- **Suggestion:** Add a sensitivity table that recomputes the key ratios and sub-period growth rates (1938–50, 1950–68, Finland ratio in 1950 and 1968) under the Table 7 bounds applied as linear drifts. Replace the generic robustness sentence at 04_limitations.tex:58 with this evidence.
- **Location:** 03_results.tex:15; 04_limitations.tex:58; 06_finland.tex:11; 05_implications.tex:37; Table 8

### MC4. The benchmarks are under-documented in the paper itself
- **Dimension:** Methodology / presentation
- **Issue:**
  - **1938.** The paper's main new benchmark gets one paragraph (02_method.tex:25). It reports a Fisher ratio of 1.79 but no Laspeyres–Paasche spread, no expenditure-group parities, no item count by group and no weights. Everything is left to the "online appendix".
  - **Consumer parity for all of GDP.** The consumer PPP is applied to investment and government spending. In a poor agrarian economy, tradable investment goods are relatively expensive, so this probably *overstates* Estonia's real level. That would understate the Finland gap. The direction should be stated, and if possible bounded.
  - **Service rebuild.** The 1938 service rebuild uses Latvian 1935 ratios for operating surplus and taxes. The independent Norkus–Markevičiūtė estimate is 11 per cent lower, yet Table 7 gives the 1938 services bound as only ±3 per cent. These need to be reconciled.
  - **1990.** Bolotin's figures are dollars from a bilateral Austria–USSR ICP-type comparison. They are taken directly as Geary–Khamis Int$1990 (02_method.tex:27; T1). Maddison himself used only Bolotin's *ratios*, applied to his own USSR total. The paper should state this assumption and point out that the 1990 cross-check against the 2011-based series (3 per cent) is what justifies it.
- **Suggestion:**
  - Add a compact table for the 1938 PPP: groups, weights, Laspeyres, Paasche and Fisher.
  - Add a sentence on the direction of the consumer-parity bias.
  - Widen or justify the ±3 per cent services bound.
  - Make the Bolotin-to-GK assumption explicit.
- **Location:** 02_method.tex:21, 25, 27; Table 2 (T1_benchmarks.tex); Table 7 row 1; 04_limitations.tex:56

### MC5. The post-1991 divergence is explained in the wrong direction, and the 1991 "within 1 per cent" match is fragile
- **Dimension:** Internal consistency
- **Issue:**
  - Table 6(b) shows the Soviet-period series *below* the modern series after 1991 (−14 per cent in 1992 to −35 per cent in 1995).
  - The text puts this partly down to trade turnover "that understate[s] inflation" (04_limitations.tex:36). Understated inflation inflates real turnover and would push the Soviet series *up*. The direction is wrong.
  - The Soviet trade index falls 43 per cent in 1991 (1,670.9 → 951.7, Table A3), across the April 1991 price reform, the point where comparable-price deflators are least reliable. The 1991 agreement used in the abstract and conclusion therefore rests on the most doubtful observation. The benchmark-year gap (1990, +3.0 per cent) is the cleaner test.
- **Suggestion:**
  - Correct the explanation: the post-1991 shortfall is consistent with *over*-deflated turnover and with unrecorded new activity.
  - Lead with the 1990 agreement (3 per cent).
  - Justify splicing at 1991 rather than 1990, or show that the choice is immaterial.
- **Location:** 04_limitations.tex:36; main.tex:118, 137; 07_conclusion.tex:20; Table 6 (T3_overlaps.tex:19–24); Table A3 trade column

### MC6. The account of the MPD's level is inconsistent, and the USSR benchmark is mismatched
- **Dimension:** Internal consistency / comparators
- **Issue:**
  - **(a) MPD 1990 level.** §I says the MPD's 1990 level "descends from Maddison's application of Bolotin's republic-to-Union ratios" (main.tex:163). §IV says the MPD and the new series share the 2011 anchor, so "the whole of the difference ... lies in the growth attributed to 1990–2010, not in the level" (04_limitations.tex:36). Both cannot explain the 32 per cent gap.
  - **(b) MPD vintages.** Table 1 uses MPD 2020 as quoted by Norkus; §IV uses MPD 2023 values (17,247 Int$2011).
  - **(c) USSR denominator.** "Estonia as % of USSR" uses an MPD Union series that implies about 6,900 Int$1990 in 1990 (8,425/1.22). Bolotin, whose benchmark anchors Estonia, puts the USSR at 6,275 (02_method.tex:29). On a consistent Bolotin basis, Estonia in 1990 is about 134 per cent of the Union, not 122, so all the USSR ratios are understated by about 10 per cent. Applying the conclusion's range "a fifth to a half richer" to a different denominator changes its meaning.
- **Suggestion:** Reconcile the MPD narrative (state which vintage and which construction applies to which year), use one MPD vintage throughout, and compute the USSR ratios on a basis consistent with Bolotin, or report both.
- **Location:** main.tex:135, 163; 04_limitations.tex:36; Table 1 (T4_previous.tex:9, 30); Table 8 USSR column; 05_implications.tex:33; 07_conclusion.tex:22

### MC7. The interwar industry index uses a different and questionable method from the Soviet one
- **Dimension:** Methodology
- **Issue:**
  - Interwar industry, 20 per cent of 1938 value-added, is measured by "employment adjusted for real wages" (02_method.tex:41; T2). This treats the consumption real wage as a productivity proxy. In the 1920s food prices were falling (the same fact behind Valge's spurious 1924 jump), so the CPI-deflated real wage rises for price reasons and overstates industrial productivity. The index grows 11.5 per cent a year in 1919–29.
  - The overlap therefore compares an employment-and-wage index (interwar) with a physical-output index (Soviet), which weakens the claim that the two series share "no sources, no weights" and are comparable.
  - Table 7 gives this proxy only ±1 per cent.
- **Suggestion:**
  - Cross-check against Valge's current-price industrial value-added deflated by an industrial wholesale price index (Klesment already used WPIs; main.tex:161).
  - Or build a physical index from the interwar yearbooks for the main products (oil shale, cement, paper, cotton cloth, electricity), as for the Soviet period.
  - Widen the Table 7 bound accordingly.
- **Location:** 02_method.tex:41; Table 3 (T2_sources.tex:14); Table 7 (T7_sensitivity.tex:14); Table 5 (industry 1919–29)

### MC8. Weights at 1990 market prices: the net-tax bound does not address distortion between sectors
- **Dimension:** Methodology (weighting)
- **Issue:** The paper bounds the effect of leaving taxes and subsidies inside the 1990 weights at 0.5 per cent, using the *net* ratio of product taxes to value-added in Bulgaria and Ukraine (02_method.tex:27; T7). The problem for the weights is *gross*: turnover tax was concentrated on light industry, food and alcohol, and large subsidies went to agriculture and meat and dairy. A net figure near zero says nothing about how sector shares are reallocated. That reallocation is exactly what Bergson's adjusted-factor-cost standard is designed to correct. Bolotin's sector parities themselves (0.40 $/rouble in agriculture against 1.80 in industry) point to large relative-price distortions.
- **Suggestion:** Use Union-level or Baltic-level turnover-tax and subsidy shares by branch (CIA AFC adjustments are available at Union level) to re-weight, and report the effect on 1950–68 and 1968–89 growth. At minimum, replace the 0.5 per cent "bound" with a scenario.
- **Location:** 02_method.tex:27; Table 7 (T7_sensitivity.tex:21)

In the same paragraph, the shortfall of "one and a half to two points" sits next to the periphery figure of 2.1.

Using 32.0 gives GVA of 656.7 and about 595 kroon per head. The gap is small (+0.5 per cent) but the table caption is wrong as it stands (02_method.tex:21; T1_benchmarks.tex:4, 17, 21).

  - Housing value-added rises 56 per cent in 1929–31 (55.2 → 86.2, Table A3) during the Depression, which is implausible for a housing stock. Yet 03_results.tex:13 cites housing growth of 6.8 per cent a year over 1929–38 as evidence of the boom.
  - The cement-based construction index swings 17.8 → 61.7 → 18.4 (1922–24).
  - The Soviet housing "stock" falls in 1959 and 1984.
  Smooth these, explain them, or flag them in the notes.
- **mc10.** Late-1980s anomalies: transport and communications rises 41 per cent over 1986–89 while the economy stagnates, then halves in 1995. "Other services" jumps 14 per cent in 1985. These feed the 1989 Soviet maximum and should be explained.
- **mc11.** Table 7 sign convention: the text says the Swedish evidence bounds the fixed-weights error "though not ... its sign" and that "the opposite sign cannot be excluded" (04_limitations.tex:48), yet the table enters a one-sided −14. The interwar margin of "about 6 per cent" (04_limitations.tex:58) has no stated aggregation rule; the root-sum-square of the interwar rows is 5.1 and the simple sum is 12.
- **mc12.** Overlap dynamics: in 1937–40 the year-to-year changes of the two series have opposite signs in two of three years (interwar −1.4, +1.9, +1.5; Soviet +3.9, −4.6, +4.3 per cent). The overlap supports levels, not annual movements. Say so. Also clarify which sources the Soviet series uses for 1937–39, since ESSR yearbooks begin in 1957; this bears on the claim that the two series "share no sources" (04_limitations.tex:34).
- **mc13.** Endpoint choice for periodisation: 1968 is the single-year maximum of the Estonia/Finland ratio (88 in 1967, 91 in 1968, 84 in 1969). The break in Estonia's own growth is robust (about 3.8 per cent over 1950–70 against 0.8 per cent over 1970–89), but the Finland "convergence then divergence" narrative should be checked with 1965/1970 or multi-year averages.
- **mc14.** Comparator groups overlap: Finland and Iceland are in both "Nordics" and "Western periphery", and Denmark, Norway and Sweden are in both "Nordics" and "Western core" (T5 notes). Comparisons of Finland against the periphery (06_finland.tex:13) are partly circular.
- **mc15.** Growth gaps read as the "cost of occupation" (06_finland.tex:13) are unconditional. A poorer Estonia would be expected to outgrow the core through convergence, so differences in raw growth rates understate the cost relative to the core. Consider conditional-convergence or synthetic-control style benchmarks, or at least acknowledge the point.
- **mc16.** Table 5 note: "Growth in 1938–50 is affected by the wartime trough of 1944". Compound growth between end years does not depend on 1944. What is meant is that the Soviet-only branch indices are linked through the war (T6_sectoral.tex:34).
- **mc17.** The abstract and conclusion call the series "unbroken" or "continuous" for "1913 and 1919–2022", which has a five-year gap (main.tex:118, 137; 07_conclusion.tex:20).
- **mc18.** "Kuboniwa's and Ponomarenko's reconstructions" cites only Ponomarenko (04_limitations.tex:42). Lithuania, the main comparator in Tables 4 and 8, comes from an unpublished manuscript and thesis (klimantas2026soviet, klimantas2025thesis). Editors may ask for these to be made available.
- **mc19.** Abstract is about 210 words and spills onto page 2 of the title page. EHR and the project target is 150.
- **mc20.** The paper has no figures. A reconstruction paper should include at least (i) the long series on a log scale with Finland and the Western core, and (ii) the two overlaps.
- **mc21.** 05_implications.tex:33: "transfers affected what the republic consumed rather than what it produced" and "its high income was inherited rather than created" are causal claims. Estonia's value-added at administered prices (underpriced energy and raw-material inputs) is itself affected by inter-republican pricing. Cite the terms-of-trade literature (e.g. Tarr 1994; Orlowski 1993) and hedge.
- **mc22.** Biber warnings: non-integer `month` fields in cia1990, adlys1988, dagys1959 and ciasovietrep1970 (main.blg). Table 3's in-cell `\cite` prints a long first citation in the cell, and "World Bank (1996)" uses author-date style inside a notes-style paper.

## Referee Objections

1. **"Your validation test cannot fail for the reasons you claim."**
   - *Why it matters:* The whole defence against Gerschenkron, fixed value-added and service-productivity bias rests on the 1937–40 overlap. That overlap is reached through a 15–25 per cent-uncertain wartime link partly borrowed from Lithuania.
   - *How to address:* Run alternative wartime links. Report the implied range of overlap gaps and of the net bias over 1940–90. Restrict the claims to net error. Add the MC3 sensitivity table for the 1950 level.

2. **"The 1938 Finland ratio compares a direct PPP for Estonia with a backcast for Finland."**
   - *Why it matters:* The title and abstract rest on 75 per cent in 1938 and on "almost half the gap" having opened by 1938.
   - *How to address:* Do a direct Estonia–Finland 1938 comparison (Finnish price data for 1938 are plentiful), or triangulate against independent 1930s PPP evidence. Report the ratio for 1937–39 averages and with a ±6 per cent level band.

3. **"Why should a consumer-price PPP, Latvian service ratios and an employment-times-real-wage industry index be trusted for 1938, when an independent estimate is 11 per cent lower?"**
   - *Why it matters:* The interwar level drives every interwar comparison.
   - *How to address:* Publish the PPP table (Laspeyres, Paasche, Fisher). Show sensitivity to the Latvian ratios. Cross-check industry with WPI-deflated value-added or a physical index. Reconcile with Norkus–Markevičiūtė explicitly.

4. **"Bolotin's dollars are not Geary–Khamis dollars, and his Union total is not the MPD's."**
   - *Why it matters:* Level comparability with Finland, the Western core and the USSR.
   - *How to address:* State the assumption. Use the 1990/2011 cross-check as justification. Compute the USSR ratios on Bolotin's own Union total, or show both.

5. **"Is 1940 really the interwar peak, or is the Soviet garrison being counted?"**
   - *Why it matters:* The 1940 level enters the splice. The 1940 rise is driven by defence and transport, while agriculture falls.
   - *How to address:* Document the 1939–40 components. Exclude occupation forces from both value-added and population, or include them consistently. State which series each 1937–40 row of Tables A2–A3 comes from.
