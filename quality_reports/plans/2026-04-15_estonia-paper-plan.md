## Contents Outline

### Abstract (~150 words)

### I. Introduction (~700 words)
- Estonia's place in European economic history: peripheral, Protestant, comparable to Finland before WWII — yet left far behind
- The gap: no reliable, internationally comparable annual GDP series for 1919–1995
- Existing attempts and their limitations (Valge 2003, Klesment 2008/2010)
- Contribution: three-dimensional reconstruction yielding a full 1913/1919–2022 series in international dollars
- Main findings: interwar growth real but Estonia lagged Finland substantially even in 1938; Soviet-era growth faster than popular narrative; transition costs severe
- Paper structure

### II. Historical Background and Existing Estimates (~800 words)

**II.1 Historical background**
- Pre-WWI Estonia as part of the Russian Empire: industrialised, Protestant, comparatively advanced
- Independence wars, land reform, and state-building (1918–1920)
- Interwar economy: agrarian reorientation, monetary stabilisation, Depression, 1930s industrialisation
- Soviet occupation: collectivisation, heavy industrialisation, demographic upheaval
- Transition (1990–1995) and restoration of independence

**II.2 Existing estimates and their limitations**
- Valge (2003): interwar GDP series — scope, PPP conversion critique, why figures need revision
- Klesment (2008) and Klesment et al. (2010): Soviet-period estimates — productive-sector bias and limited coverage
- Norkus & Markevičiūtė (2021): the 1913 anchor

### III. Data and Methodology (~2,000 words)

**III.1 The output (production) approach**
- Benchmark-extrapolation framework: why the output approach is the only viable option given data constraints; expenditure and income approaches ruled out
- Sectoral value-added indices aggregated using benchmark weights
- Evidence that the approach yields robust results; cross-reference to §V for limitations

**III.2 Dimension 1 — New 1938 interwar benchmark and the 1919–1940 series**

*(a) The 1938 benchmark*
- Critique of Valge's PPP conversion in detail
- Construction of new 1938 GDP per capita PPP benchmark: methodology analogous to Klimantas (2023) Lithuania and Klimantas et al. (2023) Latvia
- PPP conversion to 1990 and 2011 international dollars

*(b) The 1919–1940 series*
- Sectoral value-added indices: agriculture, industry, construction, trade, transport & communications, services
- Using the new benchmark to recalculate and extend Valge's figures to 1919–1940

**III.3 Dimension 2 — The Soviet-period series and its extension (1937–1995)**
*(principal new contribution of the paper)*
- The Bolotin (1990) benchmark: scope, construction, and why it provides the strongest available anchor for the occupation period
- Extension to cover 1937–1995: sectoral coverage (agriculture, industry, construction, transport & communications, trade, services)

**III.4 Splicing the series and constructing the long-run series**
- Overlap table: 1937–1940 (interwar vs. Soviet-period series) — precision as validation
- Overlap table: 1990–1995 (Soviet-period series vs. Maddison Project Database)
- Linking to Norkus & Markevičiūtė (2021) 1913 figure
- Final series: annual GDP per capita 1913/1919–2022 in 1990 and 2011 international dollars; population series

### IV. The New Estonian GDP Series (~500 words)
- Summary table of key levels and growth rates
- Comparison with Valge (2003) and Klesment et al. (2010): what changes and why
- Data appendix note ("Sources: see online appendix")

### V. Limitations and Sensitivity Analysis (~600 words)
- Single deflation bias: interwar and Soviet periods
- Late benchmark issues for the interwar series
- Service sector coverage gaps
- Soviet-era data reliability
- Sensitivity of results to benchmark choice
- Overlap precision (1937–1940 and 1990–1995) as principal robustness check

### VI. Implications: Estonian Economic Growth in European Perspective (~2,200 words)

**VI.1 What drives catch-up growth in the periphery?**
- Brief review of relevant growth theory: Gerschenkron (advantages of backwardness), Abramovitz (social capability), neoclassical convergence (Solow), endogenous growth and institutions (Acemoglu, North)
- Framework for interpreting Estonian performance across three regimes

**VI.2 Interwar Estonia (1919–1940): recovery, Depression, and the 1930s acceleration**
- Post-WWI disruption and the slow 1920s
- Navigating the Great Depression
- 1930s industrialisation drive: state-led import substitution, Denmarkisation, co-operatives
- Relative position in Eastern Europe: convergence or divergence?
- Growth theory lens: social capability, export orientation, institutional stability as enabling factors

**VI.3 Under Soviet occupation (1940–1995): growth, stagnation, and decline**
- WWII shock: demographic and economic collapse
- 1950s–1960s: Soviet growth and Baltic industrial expansion in European perspective
- 1968 onwards: deceleration — earlier and sharper in Estonia than in Lithuania
- 1970s–1980s stagnation: systemic constraints, heavy-industry bias, slowing TFP
- 1987–1995: pre-transition decline and severity of the transition shock
- Growth theory lens: Soviet growth as forced structural change without institutional modernisation; stagnation as exhaustion of extensive growth

**VI.4 A Finnish miracle that never was**
- The Estonia–Finland comparison in the literature: a recurring narrative of shared destiny interrupted by Soviet occupation
- Evidence for similarity: Protestant culture, comparable literacy, land reform, export-oriented agrarian economies
- Evidence from new figures: income levels in 1919, 1929, and 1938 — Estonia substantially below Finland; Latvia considerably closer
- Additional evidence from Datasheets: trade openness, human capital indicators, sectoral structure
- The Soviet-era divergence: real and large, but Estonia was already trailing Finland markedly in 1938
- Conclusion: qualitative affinities real but income parity overstated; gap owes as much to Finland's exceptional post-war growth as to Estonia's Soviet stagnation

### VII. Conclusion (~500 words)
- Recap of three-dimensional reconstruction and what it adds over existing estimates
- Key findings across three periods
- The Finnish comparison: a useful heuristic but not a reliable guide
- Ready for Maddison Project Database integration
- Directions for future research
