## Facts by theme

### Interwar 1913-38: recovery, depression, trade, agrarian reform, money

- By 1920 industrial output in Latvia (with Germany, France, Belgium, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Austria, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria) was at least 30 per cent below 1913; immediately after the war central and eastern European output was 50-60 per cent below pre-war. — Aldcrofteh2013, p. 19
- The entrepot trade of the Baltic ports (Riga, Reval, Narva) as suppliers to the Russian Empire "were shattered beyond repair by 1918"; the Baltic states "had lost their main Russian market". — Aldcrofteh2013, pp. 21, 35
- Twelve European countries carried out agrarian reform; the area redistributed was greatest in Latvia (about 42 per cent of territory) and Romania (30 per cent). — Aldcrofteh2013, p. 24
- Latvia stabilised its currency in 1921 at 0.8 per cent of pre-war value (Estonia 1924/1927 at 1.1 per cent); Latvia "without foreign aid, put a definite stop to inflation in the summer of 1921", ceasing recourse to the printing press in July 1921 and valorising taxes in gold. — Aldcrofteh2013, pp. 63, 65
- Latvia returned to gold in August 1922, imposed exchange control in October 1931 and did not devalue (table of gold-standard dates). — bernankegd01, p. 74
- In the later 1920s eastern Europe grew slightly faster than the west, with "impressive gains in industrial production, particularly in Hungary, Romania, Latvia, Czechoslovakia and Poland", assisted by foreign capital, subsidies, tariffs and state enterprise; over 1913-29 "the Baltic states also performed well". — Aldcrofteh2013, pp. 57-8
- Estonia and Latvia developed foreign markets for butter and bacon; Lithuania shifted from cereals to dairy and livestock "like Denmark". — Aldcrofteh2013, p. 58
- Lithuanians exported as much butter per head as the Irish and Dutch "though not as much as the Danes, the Estonians and the Latvians"; Lithuanian agricultural productivity remained lower than in the other Baltic states and was two-thirds to three-quarters of the European average in 1931-35. — lane_2001_lithuania, p. 14
- Estonia and Latvia, with the Netherlands and Denmark, "were among the largest agricultural producers in Europe per capita" (citing the Latvian Central Statistical Office's *The Baltic States*); both needed more farm workers and mechanisation was low. — olaf_stalin, p. 136 (2022 ms. pagination)
- Latvia, annual log-changes 1930-36: wholesale prices -.16, -.18, .00, -.02, -.01, +.05, +.04; industrial production +.08, -.20, -.08, +.31, +.15, +.05, +.04; real wages +.20, +.18, -.15, -.05, +.01, -.05, -.02; bank deposit/currency ratio +.03, -.57, +.11, -.06, +.12, +.10, +.45 (League of Nations data). — bernankegd01, pp. 81, 83, 85, 94
- July 1931: "Run on banks with German connections. Bank of Libau and International Bank of Riga particularly hard hit"; Latvia's banking-crisis dummy runs July 1931-June 1932, alongside Estonia's (September 1931-August 1932). — bernankegd01, pp. 91, 100
- 1934 Anglo-Baltic trade agreements obliged each state to buy a share of coal from Britain: Estonia 85 per cent, Latvia 70, Lithuania 80; the British share of Latvian imports rose from 8.4 per cent (1929) to 20.4 per cent (1935); the German share fell to 24.5 per cent in 1934 and recovered to 39 per cent by 1938, two points below 1929 (41 per cent). — hiden_salmon, pp. 90-1
- Schacht's 1934 "New Plan" and clearing agreements tied Baltic agricultural exports to blocked-account purchases of German goods; Britain's grip on Baltic trade "was never entirely loosened". — hiden_salmon, p. 93
- The 1934 Baltic Entente had no military clauses; "the very close similarity in the three states' economic structures made economic co-operation difficult". — lane_2001_lithuania, p. 35; cf. hiden_salmon, p. 180 on the 1920s Baltic League talks "blighted by the great similarity" of Latvia and Estonia
- Secret protocol of 23 August 1939: the Germans had hoped to retain southern Latvia; the Soviets pushed the line to the Latvian-Lithuanian border, and Hitler "relinquished Courland". — hiden_salmon, p. 105
- Latvian merchant fleet: 45 ships in 1920, 55 after Lithuania took Klaipėda, 124 sea-going ships in 1935 and 103 at the start of 1940. — adomavicius_2011 (passing), p. 4
- First Baltic social security programmes date from 1919; interwar systems were Bismarckian, coverage low, farmers excluded. — aidukaite_2009_transformation, pp. 99-100

### 1940-41 Sovietisation, war, population losses

- June 1941 deportations: about 10,800 from Estonia, 15,400 from Latvia, 18,000 from Lithuania (Brandes, Sundhaussen and Troebst). — feest2014, p. 83 n. 12
- Soviet-era statistical practice at annexation: Estonian industrial output in kroons was converted into 1926/27 fixed roubles by an arbitrary factor, which "delivered the desired results: a low industrial production during independence and rapid industrial growth after Sovietization. A similar method was obviously also used in Latvia and Lithuania to produce the same fairy-tale scenario"; a plausible conversion makes Estonian per-capita industrial output twice the Soviet average; output in the first five months of 1941 "might have increased by one-third or declined by one-tenth; there is no way" to know. — olaf_stalin, p. 53
- German occupation: procurement prices fixed at 60 per cent of the East Prussian level, "at least two or three times higher than under the Soviets"; the land reform reversed de facto but not de jure; after 1944 the Soviet procurement norms initially remained at German-occupation levels. — olaf_stalin, pp. 138-9
- In 1943 some 10,000 Jewish slave labourers, "mostly from Latvia and Lithuania", were sent to the Vaivara camps for the Estonian oil-shale industry; concentration-camp inmates were nearly a quarter of Estonia's industrial and mining workforce in 1944. — olaf_stalin, p. 63
- Himmler described Latvians, Lithuanians and Estonians as lower races to be largely removed to Germany; Rosenberg's 18 February 1943 decree on restoring private property in the Estonian, Latvian and Lithuanian general districts; motor vehicles withdrawn from civilian traffic from 1 March 1943. — Greska 1963 (passing), pp. 70, 215, 222-3
- Post-war border changes: Estonia ceded 5 per cent of pre-war territory and 6 per cent of population to the RSFSR; Latvia's losses "amounted to about 2 per cent", in areas inhabited predominantly by Russians. — hiden_salmon, pp. 129-30
- Deportation totals 1944-52 according to the émigré literature: 124,000 Estonia, 136,000 Latvia, 245,000 Lithuania (Vizulis 1985); "in Estonia as many as 80,000 people were deported in 1949 alone". — hiden_salmon, p. 129

### 1944-53 reconstruction, deportations, collectivisation

- Post-war land reform cut farms above 30 ha, in practice to 20 ha, "extremely rigid" by comparison with Poland, Hungary or Czechoslovakia; it was needed because the interwar reforms had already abolished the estates. — feest2014, pp. 85-6
- Share of dispossessed land left in the state land fund: Lithuania about 45 per cent, Latvia 42, Estonia 33 (peasants reluctant to take land). — feest2014, p. 86 n. 23
- Collectivisation was "strictly off-limits" until 1947 (in Latvia dismissed as "mere rumor"); Latvian communists in the mid-1940s still envisaged a "third way". — feest2014, pp. 83-4
- Small-scale farming was a source of recovery; refugees from the 1946 famine in Russia, Ukraine and Moldova bought Baltic grain and potatoes ("sack people"). — feest2014, pp. 84, 91
- Chronology: first kolkhoz in Latvia September 1945; 4 Feb 1947 Molotov tells the Latvian bureau collective farms are top of the agenda; 21 May 1947 CC decree "On founding kolkhozes in the Lithuanian, Latvian and Estonian SSR"; 12 Dec 1947 Latvian resolution defining kulaks; 26-27 Oct 1948 Latvian plenum; 9 Jan 1949 Latvian party congress decides mass collectivisation; 21 Mar 1949 Latvian Council of Ministers resolution No. 282 on deporting kulak families. — feest2014, pp. 107-9
- End of 1947: 17 model kolkhozes in Lithuania, 30 in Latvia, 5 in Estonia, heavily subsidised. — feest2014, p. 93
- Taxation from 1947 was the main coercive instrument; the December 1947 monetary reform destroyed peasants' savings and free-market prices fell by up to 50 per cent; richer peasants formed kolkhozes while smallholders held out. — feest2014, p. 96
- Farms declared kulak at end-1947: Estonia 5.3 per cent, Latvia 4.1, Lithuania 2.4. — feest2014, p. 98
- Deportations: Lithuania spring 1948 c. 43,300 (Vesna); March 1949 (Priboi) 33,496 from Lithuania, 41,445 from Latvia, 20,660 from Estonia; Lithuania 1951 another 19,000 (Osen); majority of March 1949 deportees women and children. — feest2014, pp. 99-100, 109
- By May 1949, 66 per cent of farms in Estonia and 71.6 per cent in Latvia had joined kolkhozes; Lithuania only 60 per cent by end-1949 and 90 per cent by end-1950. — feest2014, p. 100
- Estonia: Karotamm planned 25-30 per cent collectivisation for 1949; 78 per cent by October 1949; "one mass deportation was sufficient". — olaf_stalin, p. 143
- In Latvia over 200 kulaks were expelled from kolkhozes in 1948; amalgamation 1950-54 ("second collectivization"): Lithuania 6,500 kolkhozes to 1,809 by 1954, Estonia 2,213 (1950) to 937 (1952). — feest2014, pp. 101-2
- Latvian party membership 53 per cent Latvian in 1949 (Estonia 43.5 per cent, Lithuania 18.5 per cent Lithuanian on 1 Jan 1948), many of them Russian-raised. — feest2014, p. 94 n. 61
- About 100,000 Soviet-raised Latvians ("latovichi") were imported into political, administrative and managerial posts. — hiden_salmon, p. 139
- Soviet Fourth Five-Year Plan gave Lithuania the lowest investment per head of all republics; per capita investment in Lithuania was one quarter of Estonia's and one half of Latvia's; "Lithuanian industrial growth was delayed compared with that of its neighbours". — lane_2001_lithuania, pp. 67, 75, 86 n. 56
- Lithuanian agricultural output in 1951 was 60 per cent below pre-war; dairy cattle fell from 848,000 to 504,000; output did not recover for more than a decade. — lane_2001_lithuania, p. 77
- Estonian agriculture did not regain its 1938-9 level until the early 1960s, ten years after manufacturing; housing losses were not made up until 1955. — hiden_salmon, pp. 131-2
- "The recovery of the war-destroyed economy probably would not have been faster without Soviet rule" (Parming and Järvesoo), the Soviets drawing on inherited skills, education, plant and infrastructure "advanced by Soviet standards". — hiden_salmon, p. 130

### 1950-68 growth, industrialisation, immigration, sovnarkhoz, 1959 purge

- Latvian industrial development emphasised steel, agricultural machinery and motor vehicles, the heavy industries of pre-1914 Riga; Estonia oil shale, chemicals, cement, textiles; Lithuania very large plants from the 1950s. — hiden_salmon, p. 131
- Baltic specialisation within the USSR: radio and television sets, electric trains, telephone exchanges, and over-representation in tape recorders, motor bikes, sewing machines, refrigerators, textiles and shoes; total Baltic production about 3.5 per cent of the Soviet total; over 12 per cent of Soviet meat. — hiden_salmon, p. 172
- VEF in Riga was "one of the most important suppliers" of Soviet telephone equipment, "a large and long-established firm from before the Soviet takeover ... with a reputation for high quality"; it built the switching equipment of the Istok/Neva exchange and was to produce the Kvant-S rural exchange; "Latvia's separation from the USSR in 1990 must have jeopardized its sales". — campbell_1995_soviet, pp. 69, 75, 79
- In 1963 management of some Soviet branches was moved to a new North-West (Baltic) economic region comprising Leningrad and Kaliningrad oblasts, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania; Riga was to be its centre. — anusauskas_2005_lietuva, p. 433
- The sovnarkhoz system was manipulated by local administrators (Lithuania "bought" road-building funds from other regions); "the rollback of these limited freedoms took place towards the end of the 1960s". — samonis_1995_transforming, p. 6
- Moscow abandoned sovnarkhoz in 1965, returning to branch ministries; by the 1980s five-sixths of Lithuanian enterprises were controlled by all-Union or Union-republican ministries and the republic directed about 15 per cent of industry. — lane_2001_lithuania, pp. 78, 80
- Lithuania benefited from the sovnarkhoz system because it "did not undergo the centralised industrialisation that took place in Latvia and Estonia"; enterprises were sited to use local labour, avoiding a large labour-force influx, and favoured consumer goods ("no need to produce wagons, as in Latvia"). — anusauskas_2005_lietuva, p. 435 [Lithuanian original: "nepatyrė pokario metais tokios centralizuotos industrializacijos, kokia įvyko Latvijoje ir Estijoje"]
- About 400,000 Baltic young people went to the virgin lands while Russian-speaking migrants flowed into Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia. — anusauskas_2005_lietuva, p. 441
- Latvian "national communists" under deputy premier Berklavs gained control of the party in 1958 and tried to stem immigration and centralisation; Khrushchev installed Pelše, Berklavs was deported from Latvia in 1959 and hundreds purged; the charge was that they "tried to artificially hinder the objectively determined process of population resettlement"; under Voss after 1966 the Latvians "remained the most subservient to Moscow of the three Baltic Communist Parties"; the 1959 purge in Lithuania "was mild by comparison". — hiden_salmon, pp. 140-1
- Immigration relative to pre-war population 1950-89: Estonia three times, Latvia more than twice, Lithuania's level (Norgaard); explanations for heavier immigration into Estonia and Latvia: low birth rates, heavy Soviet investment in existing industries, housing-queue jumping, settlement of retired officers. — lane_2001_lithuania, pp. 73-4, 85 n. 50
- Ethnic Latvians: over 75 per cent pre-war, 57 per cent in 1970, 50.7 per cent in 1989; Riga 38.3 per cent Latvian in 1979, Jelgava the only large city with a Latvian majority (52 per cent); Latvia 62.5 per cent urban in 1970. — hiden_salmon, pp. 131-2
- Russian speakers reached 38 per cent in Estonia and 48 per cent in Latvia (about 20 per cent in Lithuania). — lane_2001_lithuania, p. 77
- Latvian birth rates "the lowest in the world"; in 1980, 1985 and 1987 more Latvians died than were born; in 1987 136,000 people moved into Latvia and 117,000 left, immigration accounting for 65 per cent of population growth; turnover among Russians about fifty leaving per hundred arriving. — hiden_salmon, p. 133
- Only Kazakhstan experienced a more radical demographic shift than Latvia. — hiden_salmon, p. 127

### 1968-89 slowdown, militarisation, living standards, net contributor

- 1985 republic indicators: fixed capital per head (roubles) USSR 5,630, Latvia 6,420, Estonia 7,270, Lithuania 5,920, Russia 6,640; retail sales per head USSR 1,168, Latvia 1,636, Estonia 1,733, Lithuania 1,393; urban housing m2 per head USSR 14.0, Latvia 16.1, Estonia 17.6; rural USSR 20.6, Latvia 32.0, Estonia 39.8 (Latvian urban share garbled by OCR). — yasin_1993_economic, p. 36
- Soviet researchers regarded housing data as "one of the best indicators of actual standards of living"; the Baltic states' "culture, their work ethic, and their traditions ... are very close to the Western European model". — yasin_1993_economic, p. 14
- In 1989 consolidated budget expenditure equalled 66.9 per cent of net material product; turnover tax on final goods meant low-priced Uzbek cotton generated revenue for the republics (Russia and the Baltics) where fabric was made. — yasin_1993_economic, p. 20
- "The three Baltic republics enjoy the highest standards of living in the Soviet Union"; Moscow tolerated Estonian idiosyncrasies "so long as productivity levels remain high". — hiden_salmon, pp. 127-8
- Estonia's per capita income was 40 per cent above the Union average "as a result of its economy's higher efficiency". — wbtransitionest, p. 1
- Lithuanian incomes about 9 per cent above the Soviet average in 1989; per capita incomes higher in Estonia and Latvia than Lithuania; infant mortality in the Baltics less than half the Soviet average; growth slowed in the 1970s. — lane_2001_lithuania, p. 79
- Shortages of food and consumer goods became evident in the Baltics after 1975. — hiden_salmon, p. 142
- By the early 1980s "almost 90 per cent of industrial concerns in the Baltic were run solely or jointly by All-Union authorities" (Vincentz, Die Welt, 1990). — hiden_salmon, p. 171
- In 1982 Lithuania exported 80 per cent of its industrial production to other republics and imported almost 90 per cent of supplies from there; the USSR's share of Estonian exports rose from 4.5 per cent in the 1920s to 98 per cent in the 1980s. — lane_2001_lithuania, p. 78; hiden_salmon, p. 174
- Soviet input prices: natural gas SUR 28 per 1,000 m3 vs USD 97 on the world market; oil SUR 30 vs USD 110 a ton; Moscow's figures for Lithuania's accumulated "debt" ranged from SUR 1bn to 500bn; Western economists (Summers) argued Moscow should pay Lithuania; the Baltics received above-average investment "mainly because the center could get much better returns" there; Gosbank confiscated resources mobilised by Lithuanian bank branches; "on a net basis, Soviet policies resulted in a drain of resources away from Lithuania". — samonis_1995_transforming, pp. 6-7
- Moscow's 1990 claims: Lithuania would have a 3.5bn-rouble deficit at world prices (1988 data); 21bn then 50bn roubles invested since 1940; Baltic governments countered with population losses, pollution and the destruction of the 1940 economies. — hiden_salmon, p. 173
- Lithuanian counter-estimates (Terleckas 1990): in the first half of 1940 Lithuania produced per head 1.9 times the USSR's meat, 2.8 times its milk, 2.7 times its pigs; industrial output doubled over 22 years; growth 1928-36 about 10 per cent a year (Baltic-émigré-style claims). — hiden_salmon, p. 174
- Lithuanian émigré estimates: Lithuania at 90 per cent or more of Finland's level in 1930, "perhaps somewhere between 10 and 40% if not lower by 1989"; "three decades of economic retardation". — samonis_1995_transforming, p. 7
- Estonia was asked for 340m roubles for the 1990 central budget against 168m in 1989; Soviet "Associations" (cartels) frustrated self-management in Latvia. — hiden_salmon, p. 175
- Lithuanian NMP in 1989 was 26 per cent above 1985 (Kaser). — hiden_salmon, p. 172
- Submarine base at Liepāja expanded; Hiiumaa and Saaremaa given over to military use; Baltic fleet HQ at Kaliningrad. — hiden_salmon, p. 134
- Baltic gold deposited in London in 1940 (about GBP 7m) was used by Britain in 1967 to settle claims against the USSR. — hiden_salmon, p. 137
- Latvian communists were better represented on the CPSU Central Committee than Estonians or Lithuanians; Pelše became a full Politburo member in 1966. — hiden_salmon, p. 141
- Calculations of costs and benefits "showed an increasing deficit as the pace of economic growth diminished" and comparisons with the West became more unfavourable in the 1980s. — lane_2001_lithuania, p. 82
- Telephone density c. 1992 (lines per 100): Lithuania 28, Estonia 22, Latvia 22, Soviet Union 14, Denmark 54. — wbtransitionest, p. 161
- Estonia 1990: industry about 45 per cent of NMP; energy use two to four times what its GDP level would predict; an estimated 10 per cent contraction of the economy in 1990; official energy statistics "very limited". — wbtransitionest, p. 123
- Electricity: Estonia generated 14.0 TWh in 1990, exporting 7.0 TWh, 48.5 per cent to Latvia; 1991 exports to Latvia 3.2 TWh; the north-west grid was dispatched from Riga; the Vireshi-Tallinn gas pipeline runs through Latvia, which holds large underground gas storage. — wbtransitionest, pp. 123, 130, 135

### 1990-95 transition collapse and 1991 splice

- 1991 inflation (retail/CPI): annual average Estonia 211.8 per cent, Latvia 226.4, Lithuania 224.7, Russia 90.4; Dec/Dec Estonia 303, Latvia 317, Lithuania 376.2, Russia 152.1; Baltic price reform preceded the other republics. — wbtransitionest, p. 3
- Estonia's 1990 trade: Latvia 6 per cent of exports and 5 per cent of imports; 94 per cent of exports inter-republican; of the 40 per cent of Estonian industrial output sold to the FSU only 2.5 per cent went to Latvia. — wbtransitionest, pp. 10, 90
- Free-market food prices 14 Jan 1992 (roubles/kg): beef fillet Tallinn 120, Riga 80, St Petersburg 320, Moscow 190; butter Tallinn 100, Riga 140, Vilnius 80. — wbtransitionest, p. 106
- GNP collapse 1990-93: 39 per cent Estonia, 48 per cent Latvia, 56 per cent Lithuania; 1994 growth of 2-5 per cent expected. — aage_1997_public, p. 96
- EBRD/WIIW real GDP index (1988 = 100): Latvia 1989 106.8, 1990 109.9, 1991 100.78, 1992 56.43, 1993 49.66 (1994-95 garbled); Estonia 103.3, (1990 garbled), 86.28, 63.84, 61.93, 65.03, 68.93; Lithuania 101.5, 96.43, 83.79, 54.47, 45.75, 46.67, 48.53; Russia 101.9, 98.23, 87.43, 69.94, 61.55, 52.32, 48.65. — kyn_1997_eastern, p. 269
- Latvian real GDP (1989 = 100): 1991 88.6, 1992 59.4 (later years garbled); budget balance +6.4 per cent of GDP 1991, -1.4 1992, +1.0 1993, -2.5 1994 (budget); revenue 37.4 per cent of GDP 1991; corporate tax fell from 49.5 per cent of revenue (1991) to 12 per cent (1994); transfers to households 48 per cent of spending in 1993. — aage_1997_public, p. 106
- Mygind: GDP fell nearly 40 per cent in all three countries in 1990-92; part reflects unrecorded private activity and under-reporting by state firms. — kyn_1997_eastern, p. 260
- "Official real GDP has also been declining more than actual GDP"; real government revenue fell 50-75 per cent. — haavisto_1997_introduction, p. 5

### Sources and quality of Soviet statistics (for 02_gap / 03_method)

- The 1941 conversion of Estonian output into "unchanged prices of 1926/27" by a crude factor produced a low interwar base and rapid post-Sovietisation growth; "A similar method was obviously also used in Latvia and Lithuania to produce the same fairy-tale scenario"; the real output change in early 1941 cannot be established. — olaf_stalin, p. 53
- Baltic governments in 1990 could not disaggregate Soviet foreign-trade figures to establish their contribution, "since the central Soviet authorities controlled statistical information". — hiden_salmon, p. 174
- Table 1 of Yasin selects indicators "to take into account the imperfections of Soviet statistics"; housing stock preferred to income. — yasin_1993_economic, p. 14
- World Bank 1992: Estonian energy statistics "very limited ... level of aggregation high"; energy balances never regularly prepared; financial indicators distorted by Soviet price and accounting practice. — wbtransitionest, pp. 123, 161
- The EBRD/WIIW series shows Latvian GDP rising 6.8 per cent in 1989 and 2.9 per cent in 1990 (official NMP-based), the growth the paper attributes to Soviet pricing. — kyn_1997_eastern, p. 269
- Official transition GDP series overstate the decline because new private activity went unrecorded and state firms under-reported (Sachs, Mygind); Kyn rejects the view that all the decline was illusory. — kyn_1997_eastern, pp. 260-1
- German occupation statistics: *Statistische Berichte für das Ostland*, published in Riga (Nr. 1/2 1942; 3/4 1942; 1/4 1943; 9/10 1943; 1/4 1944; 5/7 1944), and the Latvian SSR Central State Archive fond R-70 (Reichskommissar Ostland) are the sources of Grėska's 1941-44 industrial series for Lithuania. — Greska 1962 (passing), pp. 9, 11-12, 14; Greska 1963 (passing), pp. 62, 76, 86, 140, 147, 156, 159, 161, 226

## Gaps and caveats

- Most of the batch is about Lithuania, Estonia or the region; Latvia-specific quantitative material is concentrated in Feest 2014, Hiden and Salmon 1994, Aage 1997, Kyn 1997, Bernanke 2000, Wolf 2008, Berend 2009 and the two Aidukaite pieces. Nothing in the batch bears on 1962-3, on the 1955 and 1968 turning points, on Latvian military employment in 1985, or on the 1990 Bolotin benchmark.
- The paper's claim that Latvia's transition contraction was the deepest of the three rests on the new series; every official or EBRD-based figure in this batch (Aage p. 96, Kyn p. 269, Jeffries p. 178) ranks Lithuania's fall deeper than Latvia's. The paper already acknowledges the dispute; the sentence "on the present estimates, as on Hamilton and de Vries's, Latvia's was the deepest" may need to say explicitly that the official series disagree.
- Aage 1997 (p. 102) directly challenges the "net contributor" reading of Latvian-Union fiscal relations by pointing to implicit energy subsidies; the paper relies on `gatis_compensations` for the opposite conclusion and might acknowledge the energy-price argument (also Samonis 1995 pp. 6-7, which answers it).
- Hiden and Salmon's deportation totals (136,000 Latvia 1944-52, from Vizulis 1985) are far above Feest's archival counts; if the paper cites `hiden_salmon` for the Soviet period it should avoid these figures.
- Hiden and Salmon p. 132 give 50.7 per cent ethnic Latvians in 1989 where the paper (04_latvia) says 52 per cent; Lane p. 77 gives 48 per cent "Russian speakers" (not Russians) in Latvia. Worth reconciling with `eglite_2015_migracija`.
- Lithuania above Latvia in PPS GDP per head in 2001 (Jeffries), 2005 (Wolf) and 2008 (Aidukaite): check that the macro `\ltOVERTAKElv` and the phrase "it had stood above it in the transition years 1992--9" are consistent with these comparator datasets (the series used by those sources are Eurostat/Commission PPS, not the paper's).
- Bernanke's Table 4 implies a Latvian industrial-production fall of about 25 per cent over 1931-32 and a July 1931 banking panic; the paper's "10 per cent peak to trough" for income per head is not contradicted but the industrial and financial side of the Latvian depression was sharper than the Nordic analogy suggests.
- OCR damage: Yasin's Latvian urban share (printed "13.1"), the 1993-95 Latvian cells in Aage's Table 4.3 and Kyn's Table 11.2, Wolf's Latvian potential-growth and debt cells, and parts of the Latvian column in Aage's Table 4.1 are unreadable and were not reported.
- `olaf_stalin` is catalogued as the 2024 Peter Lang edition while the folder is the 2022 manuscript; pages cited here (24, 53, 60, 63, 136-44, 153) are manuscript pages.
- The Grėska folders point to the Riga-published *Statistische Berichte für das Ostland* (1942-44) and to Latvian SSR archive fond R-70; useful as a check on the paper's German-occupation sources but no Latvian figures were extracted from them.
- Passing-mention folders with a single reusable fact (Campbell Prospects p. 183 on the 1991 Televerket deal; Bemmann p. 316 on 44 per cent private forest; Knappe p. 99 on Lithuania's 10.1 per cent agricultural GDP share; Juska p. 9 on 2014 labour-law liberalisation) were not given bib entries; add them only if cited.
