## Facts by theme

### 1913 and the pre-war position
- Latvia was "the most industrialized part of the Russian Empire"; Riga's Latvian share rose from 23 to 42 per cent 1871–97; by 1900 the Baltic provinces had "almost eliminated illiteracy" — years_of_dependence, pp. 6, 9.
- Almost a third of tsarist Russia's exports passed through Riga, Ventspils and Liepāja in 1908–11; gross industrial output per head in Lithuania in 1912–13 was 7.6 and 9.75 times smaller than in Estonia and Latvia respectively (Vaskela) — norkusdvides, p. 182.
- Dresdner Bank (1930) estimates of national income per head at 1913–14 prices, post-war territories: Estonia 304 RM, Latvia 274 RM, Lithuania 235 RM, Russia 239 RM, Finland 310 RM; Latvia's 1926 figure 396 RM, 1928 397 RM — norkusdvides, pp. 94–5. Norkus notes Latgale (Vitebsk gubernia) dragged the Latvian average down; Courland and south Livonia alone were higher — p. 96.
- Clark (1938) national income per worker 1925–34, 1929 PPP dollars: Latvia 345, Estonia 341, Finland 380, Lithuania 207 — norkusdvides, p. 100.
- Latvia 1913: 39.71 per cent urban (43.19 excluding Latgale), 520,000 (20 per cent) in Riga; food self-sufficiency ratio 0.88, i.e. a net food importer dependent on Russian and Ukrainian grain (Skujeneeks 1927) — norkus_2025_denmarkisation, p. 10; norkus_2026_food, p. 58.
- GDP per capita in 2011 int$ (Norkus's compilation from MPD/Klimantas/Valge): Latvia 3,834 (1913), 2,964 (1922), 3,766 (1929), 4,527 (1938); Estonia 3,341, 2,470, 3,234, 4,068; Lithuania 2,650, 2,595, 2,620, 3,619; as % of US 1913: Latvia 37.9, Estonia 33.1, Lithuania 26.2 — norkus_2023_great, pp. 278–9.

### WWI losses and the 1920s reconstruction
- Latvia "suffered perhaps most from WWI; up to half of the population became war refugees"; relatively the largest urban share of the Baltic states; "in Latvia alone of the Baltic states fighting for independence, in 1919 people in many places (above all Riga) died of hunger" — norkusdvides, pp. 322–3.
- Of 2.5 million pre-war inhabitants of the future Latvian territory about 1,580,000 remained in 1920 (Plakans); Latvian army peaked at 76,394 in January 1920, 52,940 at the August 1920 peace — norkusdvides, p. 335.
- Seigniorage covered 65 per cent of Latvian state expenditure from 18 Nov 1918 to April 1920 (Wolf 2001); Finance Minister Ringolds Kalniņš (appointed March 1921) balanced the budget within a year; the lats, equal to one gold franc, was introduced as a unit of account in summer 1921, the Latvian rouble's float stopped in 1922 at 50 roubles per franc, new notes replaced roubles in 1924; the flax trade monopoly was a major revenue source — norkusdvides, pp. 314, 322.
- Soviet Russia paid Latvia 4 million gold roubles under the August 1920 peace (Estonia 15 million, Lithuania 3 million); Latvia stabilised without foreign loans — norkusdvides, pp. 323–4.
- Soviet jubilee statistics (1987): 1913–40 industrial output rose 2.6× in Lithuania, 1.3× in Estonia and only 0.9× in Latvia (i.e. below 1913); agricultural output 1.4×, 1.5× and 1.8× respectively — norkusdvides, p. 114.
- Denmark regained its pre-war GDPpc in 1920, Lithuania in 1923, Estonia in 1924 (1929 in the 2026 manuscript), Latvia only in 1930; 1913–38 annual growth: Denmark 1.56, Estonia 1.16 (1.07), Latvia 1.09, Lithuania 0.93 per cent; 1930–38: Lithuania 2.68, Latvia 2.53, Estonia 2.07, Denmark 0.95 — norkus_2025_denmarkisation, pp. 6–7; norkus_2026_food, p. 65.
- Norkus's MPD-based growth rates: Latvia 1913–38 0.67 per cent, 1922–38 2.68, 1929–89 2.40; Estonia 0.79, 3.16, 2.64; Lithuania 1.25, 2.10, 2.92; USSR 1.69, 8.18, 2.76 — norkus_2023_great, p. 290.
- Population growth 1920–39: Lithuania +22 per cent, Latvia +12.2, Estonia +1.5 (Kirk/League of Nations) — norkusdvides, p. 20. Natural increase per 1,000 in 1939: Lithuania 8.7, Latvia 4.6, Estonia 1.3 — p. 365.

### Agrarian reform
- Estonia and Latvia carried out "perhaps the most radical and consistent land reforms of the interwar period"; Latvia's 1920 law: state land fund 2,668 thousand ha (51.48 per cent of territory), 54,360 new farms, 51,044 smallholders enlarged, 9,980 former tenants; redemption over 41 years at about half of 1937 market value; reform declared complete 1937; German landlords left 50 ha; compensation paid for only 11 per cent of expropriated land at ≤10 per cent of market price (1924 referendum had rejected compensation) — norkusdvides, pp. 358–9 (same in norkus_2012_agrariniu, pp. 18–19).
- Landlords owned 48.1 per cent of land in Latvia, 46.9 in Estonia, 26 in Lithuania (1919); average estate 2,500 ha in Latvia, 2,113 in Estonia, 490 in Lithuania — norkusdvides, pp. 348, 361.
- Average allotment to recipients: Latvia 12.14 ha, Estonia 11.54, Lithuania 6.24; farms over 20 ha among new farms: Estonia 24.9 per cent, Latvia 13.4, Lithuania 1.4; average peasant farm Estonia 23.2 ha (1930), Latvia 20.18 (1929), Lithuania 15.03 (1930); about 30,000 applicants in Latvia received no land — norkusdvides, pp. 362, 363 (norkus_2012_agrariniu, p. 22). Roszkowski: "After the land reform Latvia became a country of medium and large peasant holdings capable of commercialising and modernising" — p. 362.
- All three Baltic states were exceptions to the post-reform output declines seen elsewhere in Eastern Europe; after the reform Latvia and Estonia "lacked hired farm labour", imported annually from Lithuania and Polish-held Vilnius — norkusdvides, pp. 364–5.
- Before the reform peasants owned 39 per cent of Latvian land; beneficiaries approached 100,000; in the early 1920s new farms produced food for self-consumption and earned cash from flax, so Latvia's food self-sufficiency ratio stayed below 1 until 1930 and grain imports were "a major political issue"; the 1928 crop failure delayed the crossing — norkus_2025_denmarkisation, pp. 10–11.
- Latvia in 1930 exported 18,430 t butter (Estonia 13,900), 16,816 t in 1933 — norkus_2025_why, p. 19.

### Interwar growth, structure and the Depression
- Manufacturing employed 17.4 per cent of Estonia's labour force in 1930, 13.5 of Latvia's, 6 of Lithuania's — years_of_dependence, p. 11.
- Latvian employment 1930 (Skujenieks 1938): agriculture 66.2 per cent, industry 14.4, services 20.3; urban share 28.2 per cent (1935) — norkusdvides, p. 496.
- Annual GDPpc in 1990 GK$ (Klimantas et al. 2023 for Latvia; Valge for Estonia): Latvia 1920 1,554; 1929 2,349; 1930 2,538; 1931 2,316 (−8.75%); 1932 2,288 (−1.21%); 1933 2,500; 1937 3,031; 1938 3,099; 1939 3,132; Estonia 1930 2,330, 1932 2,110, 1938 2,745; Lithuania never declined 1929–38 — norkus_2025_why, p. 4. Compound growth 1923–38: Latvia 3.24 per cent, Estonia 2.81, Finland 3.36, Lithuania 1.18 — p. 7.
- Latvian trade openness ((X+M)/GDP): 23.65 per cent (1922), 49.23 (1929), 45.71 (1930), 37.44 (1931), 20.02 (1932), 16.96 (1934), 30.22 (1937), 27.10 (1938), 25.66 (1939); exports/GDP 21.2 (1929), 13.55 (1938); trade balance positive in only 4 of 19 years — norkus_2025_why, p. 10.
- Public debt/GDP Latvia: 8.75 per cent (1929), 12.77 (1931), 7.57 (1935), 10.14 (1939); Estonia 22.7–34.4 per cent; Lithuania 3–8 per cent — norkus_2025_why, p. 15.
- Riga wholesale price index (1913=100): 120 (1929), 85 (1931–2), 83 (1934), 87 (1935), 113 (1937–8) — norkus_2025_why, p. 16.
- Latvia introduced exchange controls on 8 Oct 1931 and devalued the lats in September 1936 (Estonia June 1933, Finland Oct 1931, Lithuania never) — norkus_2025_why, p. 12; the 1936 devaluation was "about 40 per cent" and the lat already traded below par on the black market — norkusdvides, p. 112.
- Latvia's 1930 trade: Germany 37.2 per cent of imports, 26.6 of exports; UK 8.5 and 28.4 — norkus_2025_why, p. 11.
- Estonia and Latvia had inherited or rebuilt food-export industry in the 1920s; the state grain monopolies of both countries diverted land from fodder to wheat and rye and slowed "Denmarkisation"; Baltic food self-sufficiency ratios 1938: Lithuania 1.28, Estonia 1.21, Latvia 1.16 (Denmark 2.18) — norkus_2025_denmarkisation, pp. 9, 12–13.
- Latvian national income (Ceichners): 960 million lats in 1927, 500 lats per head; later official series per head: 444 (1933), 462 (1934), 459 (1935), 493 (1936), 585 (1937), 637 lats (1938) (Reichskommissar 1942: 21); Lithuania's 1938 per-head figure ≈ 66 per cent of Latvia's at the 1938 lat rate; "until WWII Latvia remained (Finland apart) the most developed East Baltic state" — norkusdvides, p. 112.
- Kalniņš (1947) comparative statistics: Latvia first in Europe in per-head meat (85 kg) and dairy (566 l) consumption, third in railway km per 10,000; university students per 10,000: Latvia 30.1, Estonia 28.8, Lithuania 15.0 — norkusdvides, p. 102.
- Illiteracy (Darbiņš & Vītiņš 1947): Sweden 0.1 (1931, 15+), Finland 0.9 (1930, 15+), Estonia 3.9 (1934, 10+), Latvia 11.1 (1935, 10+), Lithuania 32.7 (1923) — norkusdvides, p. 103. Infant mortality 1934–6 per 100 live births: Latvia 8.5, Estonia 9.0, Finland 6.9, Lithuania 13.9 — p. 104.
- Real wages: unskilled construction workers in Kaunas earned more than in Riga from 1932 and in 1938 "around 30% higher than in Latvia (Riga), and 64% higher than in Estonia", consistent with Vaskela (2007), although "by 1938, Latvia was the first, its GDP per capita (at purchasing power parity) surpassing Lithuania's by about 30%, closely followed by Estonia" — RefWorks:doc:5f732eb5e4b07544d3f66f7d, pp. 50–3; Vaskela: skilled male wages 16 per cent higher in Lithuania than Latvia, unskilled 11 per cent higher in Latvia — p. 52.
- Ulmanis after 1934 "pursued a policy of enlarging the state sector of the economy, primarily at the expense of the German minority" — years_of_dependence, p. 13; in Latvia "all important branches of the economy were monopolised or controlled by the state, and private enterprise remained only as a supplement" (Pistohlkors) — norkusdvides, p. 214.
- Lithuania 1938 at 2,100±100 GK$ was about 76.5 per cent of Estonia's 2,745; the Lithuania–Estonia gap in 1938 comparable to the Poland–Lithuania gap in 1990 — norkusdvides, p. 111.

### 1939–41 Sovietisation
- Under the October 1939 pact 30,000 Soviet troops were stationed in Latvia (25,000 in Estonia), more than the national armies (20,000 and 16,000); about 65,000 Germans repatriated from Latvia and Estonia in late 1939, 80,000 in all by 1941 — years_of_dependence, pp. 15–16, 41.
- "The Latvian lat, which had officially been worth 10 rubles, was made equal in value"; Baltic currencies abolished late 1940; bank accounts frozen, withdrawals limited to 1,000 roubles; by Nov 1940 Latvian industrial money wages tripled vs 1939, by May 1941 food prices had tripled and textiles/shoes risen sixfold — pp. 32–3.
- Expropriation of factories >20 workers (or >100,000 lats turnover) from late July 1940; in early 1941 only 4 per cent of Latvian light-industry managers had college education, 64 per cent primary only — pp. 31–2.
- Land above 30 ha confiscated: about 15 per cent of Latvian farms affected; 52,000 landless received land and 23,000 small farms enlarged, maxima 10 ha; about 50 MTS in Latvia by early 1941; first kolkhoz near Augšpils a week before the German attack — pp. 35–6.
- June 1941 deportation: "662 boxcars containing 15,081 individuals (of whom 3,332 were children under 16) were dispatched from Latvia"; total first-year losses about 35,000 (1.5–2 per cent of population) — p. 42.

### WWII, occupation and population losses
- German occupation: mark valued at 10 roubles (26 June 1941); property not restored; city wages and farm delivery prices set at 60 per cent of East Prussian levels — pp. 53–4.
- 126,000 Baltic workers sent to Germany by July 1944, perhaps 35,000 Latvians (esp. Latgale); "The total killed or deported by the Nazis from Latvia alone has been estimated at 120,000, half of them Jews" — p. 56.
- Up to 150,000 Latvians inducted into German forces; ≥50,000 killed, wounded or missing; 80,000 captured by Soviets (mainly Courland, May 1945); 20,000 reached the West — p. 59.
- Latvian Jews 93,000 in 1939; up to 5,000 deported June 1941, 18,000 evacuated/drafted; "Of approximately 70,000 Jews who remained in Latvia after the Soviet retreat, only about 4,000 survived" — pp. 61, 64.
- Abrene district ceded 1945: "about 2 per cent of the pre-war territory and population" — p. 74.
- Per capita war losses estimated at 10,000 roubles in Latvia (6,000 Lithuania, 15,000 Estonia); Baltic states lost about 20 per cent of population in WWII (incl. flight and territory), about 9 per cent premature deaths — pp. 74–5.
- 30,000 (Estonian radio, late 1944) and 38,000 (Soviet Latvian sources, early 1945) Baltic ex-German-army men sent to "labour service"; about 60,000 deported from Latvia in 1945–6 — p. 73.
- Population contraction bottomed out in 1945: Latvia −23.6 per cent, Estonia −20.81 (Klimantas 2025); Latvia surpassed its 1939 population in 1954–6, Estonia 1953–5, Lithuania 1969 — norkus_2026_food, p. 150.
- Estonian and Latvian fertility was already at replacement level before WWI; mobilisation into German formations meant "most males of the younger generation dying on the battlefields, escaping to the West ... or imprisoned" — norkus_2026_food, p. 152.

### 1944–53: reconstruction, deportations, collectivisation
- Latvian state-land fund rose from 875,252 ha (1940) to over 1,500,000 ha (1944); 42 per cent still undistributed at end-1945; 41 state farms, 50 MTS, 445 horse-renting points created 1944–6; 50 Latvian MTS had 400 tractors at end-1945 — years_of_dependence, p. 95.
- Kulak income tax 40 per cent of assessed income in 1947, 75 per cent in 1948; first post-war kolkhozes in Latvia November 1946; at the beginning of 1949 about 8 per cent of Latvian farms collectivised (3.9 Lithuania, 5.8 Estonia) — pp. 97–9.
- "In a few days after 20 March 1949, about 60,000 individuals were deported from Estonia and at least 50,000 from Latvia. In Lithuania ... 40,000 joined the earlier groups on 24–27 March 1949"; "During the last ten days of March 1949, the Baltic nations lost about 3 per cent of their native populations" — p. 99.
- "In Latvia, the percentage of farms collectivized jumped from about 11 per cent on 12 March to more than 50 per cent on 9 April ... By the end of the year, 93 per cent of the Latvian and 80 per cent of the Estonian farms were collectivized"; Lithuania 62 per cent at end-1949; by end-1950 only 4 per cent of Latvian farms private; 98.4 per cent collectivised December 1951 — pp. 102–3.
- Latvian kolkhozes averaged 48 households at end-1949; mergers cut their number from 4,169 (May 1950) to 1,792 (end-1950) — p. 107.
- Latvian grain output 1,372,000 t (1940), 732,000 (1950), 436,000 (1956); 1950 output "already well below that of 1940, and probably also below that of 1948"; sown area fell to about 1958 — pp. 106–7. Late 1950s agricultural output about 75 per cent of pre-war "and possibly much lower"; Latvian cultivated area 1.9 million ha in 1960 "about half that of 1939"; yields half pre-war; 93→113 Latvian sovkhozes March–October 1958, only 32 profitable — p. 190.
- Collectivisation "reduced the output of both crops and animal husbandry to some 60%–70% of the prewar level"; return to 1938 levels "in the early 1960s"; from 1963–4 Moscow imported grain and allocated much of it as fodder to the Baltic republics, which became suppliers of animal products to Moscow and Leningrad — norkus_2025_denmarkisation, p. 15.
- Industrialisation plans and investment in Latvia "exceeded the average for the USSR"; Riga Electrical Machine Plant (1947), Riga Diesel Plant (1949); lumber 1950 +55 per cent over 1938, textiles +27, paper +77 — p. 109. Industrialisation "was conducted at twice the all-Union rate in Latvia and Estonia"; in Lithuania below all-Union level — p. 110.
- Index critique: 1947 industrial output claimed 28 per cent above "Soviet Latvia 1940", "However, judging from data in natural production units, the 1937 level had not yet been reached by 1947"; the 1940 base covers only the five months of Soviet Latvia, so "An index pegged to the production of the whole calendar year 1940 would be ... roughly 40 per cent of the index pegged to the Soviet Latvia of 1940" — p. 109. "The pre-war level of industrial production was certainly reached by 1949 in Latvia and Estonia, and by 1952 in Lithuania. By 1953 Baltic industrial employment was double the pre-war level" — p. 113. Estonian oil-shale output per worker 494 t (1939) vs 482 t (1950) against claimed productivity gains — p. 113.
- Immigration: "About 400,000 Russians and 100,000 people of other nationalities migrated into Latvia from 1945 to 1959, most of them probably before 1953. This amounted to 25 per cent of the pre-war population"; Riga 1951 population 43 per cent above 1939; Latvians about 83 per cent of population in 1945, about 60 per cent by 1953 — p. 112.
- "Latvia lost at least 150,000 natives through deportations, executions and guerrilla warfare"; wartime and postwar losses "about 30 per cent of the pre-war population through death, deportation and flight ... Colonization added an equal number" — p. 129.
- Latvian forest brothers 10,000–15,000 at peak, perhaps 40,000 in all; resistance "largely crushed" by end-1949 — pp. 83, 86, 93.
- Passports were issued to all Latvian rural inhabitants only in 1947 (Lithuania 1945) — norkusdvides, p. 352.

### 1953–68: fast growth, sovnarkhoz, 1959 purge, immigration
- "In 1956, central Moscow ministries had been in charge of 90 per cent of Latvia's industrial output"; the 1957 Latvian sovnarkhoz took 486 enterprises; recentralisation began 1962, sovnarkhozy abolished 1965 — years_of_dependence, pp. 187–8.
- Latvia saw "a sharp upsurge of immigration in 1956 ... a net influx surpassing that of the six preceding years combined"; Latvians peaked at 62.0 per cent at the 1959 census — pp. 139–40.
- The autonomists expanded medical services, housing, pensions and rural consumer goods in 1957–8 and "offered Moscow increased deliveries of commodities in exchange for more autonomy" (Dzērve) — p. 142. Berklāvs dismissed 15 July 1959 for opposing heavy industry and arguing "Latvian products should first satisfy the local demand"; Pinksis objected to transferring skilled workers out and doubted finding 10,000–12,000 rural workers for the Seven-Year Plan; Kalnbērziņš and Lācis replaced November 1959 — pp. 142–3. Dzērve and Treijs (Institute of Economics) forced out 1960; "several thousand party members were expelled within less than two years" — p. 145. Indigenous share of party members 1967: Latvia 45 per cent, Estonia 52, Lithuania 66 — p. 146.
- About half of Latvian graduating specialists were dispatched outside the republic annually (1956 complaint) — p. 134.
- Baltic republics, 2.8 per cent of Soviet population, produced 3.6 per cent of gross output in the late 1960s, including over half of Soviet electric railway coaches and telephones (Latvia), 10–50 per cent of radios, motorcycles (Latvia); Baltic visible exports exceeded imports by about 10 per cent — p. 184.
- Official per capita national income relative to USSR average: 1958 Latvia +29, Estonia +19, Lithuania −8 per cent; 1968 Latvia +42, Estonia +44, Lithuania +15; 1958–68 per capita income rose 67 per cent USSR, about 90 Latvia/Estonia, 108 Lithuania — p. 185.
- Latvian urban industrial families in 1961 consumed 30–50 per cent less meat, eggs and dairy than pre-war; consumer-durable consumption regained the pre-war level only in the 1960s; "Introduction of the Soviet system ... set back its growth in welfare by two decades" — pp. 185–6.
- Industrial production 1970 vs 1955: Lithuania 6.2×, Latvia 4.7×, Estonia 4.2×, USSR 3.7×; growth "slowed down somewhat during the 1960s ... increasing industrial maturity"; c. 1961 every fourth "Latvija" radio and every third telephone from VEF defective — p. 189.
- Share of workforce in agriculture 1968: Estonia 22, Latvia 24, Lithuania 24, USSR 27 per cent; industrial employment surpassed agricultural around 1960 in Latvia and Estonia — p. 192. Birthrate low of 13.5 per 1,000 in Latvia in 1967 — p. 193. Net immigration in the early 1960s "almost 1 per cent of the existing population each year"; Latvians 57 per cent in 1970 — p. 194.
- Maddison 1973 GDP per capita (1990 GK$): Estonia 8,657, Latvia 7,846, Lithuania 7,593, USSR 6,059, Russia 6,582 — norkusdvides, p. 71 (2011$: Latvia 12,506 = 46.7 per cent of US — norkus_2023_great, p. 279).

### 1968–89: slowdown, recentralisation, militarisation, net contribution
- "In Latvia only 3 per cent of industrial production had been under Union control in 1960. By 1971 the Union ministries controlled 34 per cent, and Union-republic ministries accounted for 56 per cent, leaving only 10 per cent (mostly food and light industry) under Latvian control" — years_of_dependence, p. 228.
- Latvian immigration peaked in 1973–4 at a net 15,000 a year against natural increase of about 2,000 Latvians and 4,000 non-Latvians; Latvians 62 per cent (1959), 57 (1970), 54 (1979); Riga 44.7 (1959), 40.9 (1970, Russians 42.7) — pp. 215–16. Five-year immigration into Latvia (thousands): 71 (1965–9), 64 (1970–4), 46 (1975–9), 45 (1980–4); population 2,521k (1979), 2,604k (1985); Latvians 53.7 → 52.6 per cent — pp. 281–2; about 16,000 Latvians emigrated to the RSFSR in 1970–9 — p. 282.
- Industrial employment peaked: Latvia 34.3 per cent (1970) → 33.1 (1976); service employment the growth sector; Riga 835,000 (1979) — p. 217.
- Industrial growth in the late 1960s slightly above the Soviet average in Latvia and Estonia; in the 1970s "falling below the Soviet average (which itself was decreasing) in Latvia and Estonia"; produced national income growth 1965–75 close to the Soviet average — p. 235. Official annual growth, Latvia: national income 5.8 (1970–5), 3.6 (1975–80), 3.2 (1980–5); industrial production 6.4, 3.7, 3.2; population 0.8, 0.5, 0.6 — p. 288.
- "The United Nations estimates put real growth at about 2 percentage points lower than the official Soviet figures ... Thus, an officially reported 3 per cent growth ... represented stagnation in real per capita national income" — p. 289.
- Heavy industry's share in Latvian industry rose from 50 per cent (1965) to 57 (1977) while Estonia's consumer goods surpassed heavy industry in 1973; Latvia produced about 30 per cent of Soviet electric railway cars, 50 per cent of motorcycles/mopeds, 20 per cent of radios — p. 234. In 1973, 106 of 185 Latvian production units checked made substandard goods — p. 235.
- Soviet sources c. 1970 claimed Baltic per capita national income among Europe's highest (Estonia ahead of Denmark), but Baltic visitors to the West "felt that their people had markedly lower purchasing power", NI per head "certainly some 50 per cent above the Soviet average" — p. 235.
- Labour shortage: Latvian industrial employment did not increase in 1970–2; 25–32 per cent of basic equipment in Latvian machine plants idle 1967–71 for lack of workers; planning deputy Āboliņš protested Union-ministry plans adding "an unrealistic 13,000" workers; 70,000 farm workers seasonally underemployed; 40,000 Latvians officially on two jobs in 1973 — pp. 236–7.
- Latvia received 42 per cent of its 1970 energy from Lithuania and Estonia; Pļaviņas hydro station built — p. 239.
- Hidden inflation: the only published price index, for kolkhoz markets in four Latvian cities, rose 1 per cent a year 1965–70, 4 per cent 1970–5, 13 per cent 1975–7 — p. 221. Living standards "slowed down and probably even went into reverse" in the late 1970s — p. 210.
- "After 1975 ... shortages of the goods themselves became increasingly frequent and acute, especially in Latvia. By 1979 people from Riga were to be found trying to purchase butter in Tallinn"; Latvian urban living space per head had not exceeded the 1940 level by 1978 — p. 222. Meat shortage led to a Riga dock workers' strike in May 1976 — p. 253.
- Collective farmers' income (incl. plots) exceeded urban workers' in Latvia by 1972; private plots gave 44 per cent of Latvian collective-farm income in 1970 on 5–7 per cent of arable land; milk per cow 1970: USSR 2,110 kg, Latvia 2,950, Denmark 3,950 — pp. 230–3.
- Abortions 60,000 vs 34,000 births in Latvia 1973; alcohol deaths estimated 1,500 in 1972, a third of male deaths aged 20–50 — pp. 224–5. Male life expectancy stagnated or declined in Latvia and Lithuania in 1973–89 — norkus_2023_great, pp. 321, 349. Latvian male conscript height 177.60 cm (1959/77 cohort), 178.04 (1971/89), 178.20 (1989/2007) — p. 340.
- Nuclear submarines reported at Liepāja (1978); "the developed Baltic area supplied very few officers to the Soviet army" — pp. 226–7. The 1971 "Letter of the Seventeen Communists": Latvians only 42 per cent of LaCP CC staff, 18 per cent of Riga party members — p. 262.
- Official per capita savings 1,000 roubles (Estonia) by mid-1980s; food shortages "alleviated in the early 1980s"; Riga treated only 4.5 per cent of waste water in 1983 — pp. 285, 293.

### 1987–91: renaissance and the end of the Union
- Pļaviņas/Daugavpils hydro project: 700 letters with 30,000 signatures to Literatūra un māksla Oct–Nov 1986; USSR Council of Ministers cancelled it 7 Nov 1987 — pp. 304–5. 23 Aug 1987 Riga demonstration over 10,000, "probably the largest unofficial demonstration up to that time" in the USSR — p. 308.
- In 1988 Lithuania trailed Estonia by 3.5 months and Latvia trailed Lithuania by 1.5 months on eleven dated developments — p. 312. Popular Front of Latvia founded 8–10 Oct 1988, 110,000 members (one-third LaCP); 14 June 1988 Riga rally 50,000–100,000; 23 Aug 1988 60,000 — pp. 316, 318.
- Economic self-management laws were "largely ineffective"; "By 1989, almost all consumer goods could be classified as scarce"; Latvian sovereignty declaration 28 July 1989 — pp. 326–7. "Economic autonomy" from 1 Jan 1990 restricted autonomy in practice (savings accounts centralised) — p. 329.
- March 1990 elections: 120 of 170 first-round winners pro-independence; non-Latvians 48 per cent of population, many pre-war citizens; declaration on renewed independence (printed as 8 May 1990 in the text; actual date 4 May 1990 per norkus_2023_great, p. 237) — pp. 331, 334–5.
- Survivors in 1990: 7.29 per cent of Latvia's population aged ≥15 in 1934, 22.23 per cent born 1934 or earlier; Latvia restored the 1922 Constitution (4 May 1990, fully 6 July 1993) and 1937 Civil Code — norkus_2023_great, pp. 15, 177, 237.

### 1990–95 transition
- Maddison GDP per capita (1990 GK$): Latvia 9,916 (1990), 8,707 (1991), 5,992 (1992), 5,413 (1993), 5,624 (1994), 5,656 (1995), 7,742 (2000), 11,925 (2005), 14,724 (2007), 11,865 (2009), 11,898 (2010); Lithuania 8,663 (1990), 5,399 (1993), 4,884 (1994), 11,004 (2010); Estonia 10,820 (1990), 8,050 (1994), 19,032 (2010); World Bank 2005 PPP$: Latvia 10,108 (1990), 5,921 (1993), 15,946 (2012); Lithuania 12,499 (1990), 18,776 (2012) — norkusdvides, pp. 73–4. Annual growth 1990–2010 (Maddison): Latvia 0.87 per cent, Lithuania 1.15, Estonia 2.73; 1990–2012 (WB): Latvia 2.1, Lithuania 1.79 — p. 74.
- Latvia and Lithuania "where, because of especially unfavourable initial conditions, macroeconomic dynamics throughout the first decade of transformation had rather the shape of an L curve"; Latvia's "initial economic conditions were especially unfavourable owing to socialist super-industrialisation" and "timely financial aid from international financial organisations ... to maintain currency stability" after the 1995–6 banking crises helped most — norkus_2008_isejimo, pp. 383, 390.
- Latvian rouble issued 7 May 1992, sole legal tender July 1992, replaced by the lats March 1993, pegged to the SDR February 1994; Latvia and Estonia had stabilised their currencies by autumn 1992 while Lithuania delayed — norkusdvides, pp. 312–13, 318. The Bank of Latvia chose "the highest possible nominal exchange rate", 1 lats ≈ 2 USD — norkus_2018_glory, p. 9.
- Lithuanian privatisation "much faster and more comprehensive ... one of the fastest in the East. Already at the end of 1992, 56 per cent of all assets in Lithuania had been privatised" (Mygind) — norkusdvides, p. 328.
- All three suffered the Vanek–Reinert effect; "When foreign capital arrived in Latvia and Lithuania, the enterprises with technological capabilities in middle- to high-tech level manufacturing were already bankrupt"; Estonian electronics firms became TNC subsidiaries early — norkus_2012_between, p. 263.
- Decollectivisation: individual farms' share of gross agricultural output rose from 28 per cent (1990) to 77 per cent (1999) in Latvia (Estonia 31→54, Lithuania 32→81); Latvian decollectivisation "ended with results that should be considered a catastrophe if conventional productionist criteria were applied" — norkusdvides, pp. 387, 393; norkus_2012_agrariniu, p. 45.
- Estonia and Latvia "lost food self-sufficiency (FSSR1 = 1) during the first restored independence decade"; Latvia "remained a net food importer until the Great Financial Crisis", with the largest decline, to FSSR1 = 0.48 in 2003 — norkus_2025_denmarkisation, p. 14; norkus_2026_food, p. 95.

### 1995–2024: boom, crash, divergence
- Latvia GDPpc, EU27 = 100: 36 (1998), 37 (2000), 56 (2007), 57 (2008), 49 (2009); Estonia 42, 45, 69, 67, 62; Lithuania 40, 39, 59, 62, 53 — oecd_2011_estonia, p. 104.
- GDP growth, Latvia: 11.9 (2006), 9.9 (2007), −3.6 (2008), −14.3 (2009), −3.8 (2010), 6.4 (2011); Estonia −5.4, −14.7, 2.3; Lithuania 2.6, −14.8, 1.6 — norkus_2018_glory, p. 4. Unemployment in Latvia up to 20 per cent; trade deficits 30–40 per cent of GDP and current-account deficits 15–22 per cent before 2008 — norkus_2012_between, p. 265.
- Parex bail-out Nov 2008 forced Latvia to accept IMF tutelage, which Estonia and Lithuania avoided; Harmony Centre the main party advocating devaluation; an economist (Smirnovs) questioned by counter-espionage for discussing devaluation — norkus_2018_glory, pp. 11–12. "up to seven–eight per cent of the population of Latvia and Lithuania emigrated in 2008–11" — p. 14. Euro adoption: Estonia 2011, Latvia 2014, Lithuania 2015 — p. 13.
- Gini 2009: Latvia 37.4 (highest in EU), Lithuania 35.5, Estonia 31.4 — norkus_2012_between, p. 244. FDI stock 2007: Estonia 79 per cent of GDP (12,347 USD per head), Latvia 39 per cent (4,586), Lithuania 38 per cent (4,325) — p. 262. "Latvia made no progress to modern services, displaying a decline in industry" 2000–5 — p. 264. Latvia and Lithuania classed as semi-peripheral, Estonia semi-core — p. 269.
- "The exception is Latvia, whose economic development in the post-communist period was not more successful than Lithuania's"; the gap with Estonia "now separated from this Baltic Lombardy both Baltic Sicilies or Calabrias – Lithuania and Latvia" — norkusdvides, p. 113. In 2010 Lithuania (11,004 GK$) was ahead in Europe only of Latvia, Bulgaria, Romania and most ex-Yugoslav republics — p. 112.
- GDPpc 2011$: Latvia 21,042 (2007), 24,313 (2018, 43.9 per cent of US); Lithuania 20,880 (2008), 27,371 (2018, 49.5); Estonia 26,178 (2007), 27,409 (2018) — norkus_2023_great, p. 279. Latvia's male life expectancy 1989–2018 rose only modestly; UN forecasts no reduction of its lag behind Japan by 2050 — pp. 327, 331.
- Prison population per 100,000 (c. 2009): Latvia 288, Estonia 259, Lithuania 234, Slovenia 65 — norkus_2012_between, p. 272.

### Sources and quality of Soviet statistics
- Soviet indices pegged to "1940" in fact cover only the five months of Soviet rule and omit small private shops; "Soviet statistics invariably start with 1940"; physical-output data contradict official claims (1947 industry; oil-shale productivity) — years_of_dependence, pp. 109, 113.
- "While officially there was no inflation", kolkhoz-market indices showed 4–13 per cent a year in the 1970s — p. 221. UN discounts official growth by about 2 points; "Hidden inflation played its part, mainly in the form of manipulation of the consumer price index by the central authorities and manipulation of the producer goods price and quantity by enterprises" — p. 289.
- Official immigration figures for the Muuga harbour project ("an alleged total of 100 persons") show that "This was no way to build credibility" — pp. 290–1.
- Inter-republic food deliveries remained classified until the late Soviet era; late-Soviet data hard to interpret because food had no market prices — norkus_2025_denmarkisation, p. 14.
- Maddison's dataset gives the Baltic states only from 1973 (one point) and 1990; Norkus's own 1913/1938 estimates for Lithuania are "prolegomena"; MPD growth rates for Latvia 0.87 per cent 1990–2010 — norkusdvides, pp. 66–7, 71, 74.
- Interwar Latvian statistics were "almost the best" in the Baltic; interwar Lithuanian censorship forbade publishing comparative Baltic economic indicators showing Lithuania's lag, while Latvian jubilee volumes (Skujenieks 1938; Darbiņš & Vītiņš 1947) always compared with neighbours — norkusdvides, pp. 102, 112.

## Gaps and caveats

- Misiunas & Taagepera's statistical tables (Tables 1–16: population, industrial production 1940–80, agricultural output, immigration, housing) are in Appendix B, which is not in the folder set; the text cites them repeatedly. Several quantitative claims above (e.g. "Table 8", "Table 16") therefore rest on the text's summaries only.
- M&T 1993 deportation and population figures pre-date the archival work of the 2000s. The paper's March 1949 figures (>40,000 Latvia, c. 30,000 Lithuania, c. 21,000 Estonia) differ from M&T's (≥50,000, 40,000, 60,000); the Estonian figure in M&T looks like a typo for about 20,000 but is printed as 60,000 on p. 99. Worth a footnote that older Western estimates ran higher.
- The literacy contradiction (Finland 0.9 per cent illiterate at 15+ in 1930 vs Latvia 11.1 per cent at 10+ in 1935, Darbiņš & Vītiņš 1947 as quoted in norkusdvides p. 103) is serious for 05_baltic's "Latvian literacy was above Finnish". The Latvian figure is pulled up by Latgale and uses a lower age cut; the paper should either cite a source for the claim or restrict it to Courland/Livonia or to 1897/1920 data. The university-enrolment figure also needs reconciling (30.1 per 10,000 in Kalniņš 1947 vs the paper's 4.3 per thousand).
- Trade-openness figures: norkus_2025_why (Table 4, built on the same Klimantas et al. 2023 GDP) gives (X+M)/GDP of 49 per cent in 1929 and 27 per cent in 1938 for Latvia; the paper's "38 to 22 per cent" must be a different ratio (perhaps exports-plus-imports over GDP at a different price base, or a different end year). Check Table T9_structure definitions.
- Estonia's 1931–3 downturn on Valge's figures as reproduced by Norkus is about 9 per cent (2,330 → 2,110), not 8; and the gold-based kroon dates from 1924 in Norkus's reading (1928 is the currency reform/renaming). Minor.
- Dresdner Bank's 1913–14 national income per head puts Estonia (304 RM) above Latvia (274 RM), against the paper's "richest ... Latvia" in 1913; Norkus attributes the low Latvian average to Latgale. The paper's Norkus & Markevičiūtė 2021 benchmark (reproduced in norkus_2023_great as 3,834 vs 3,341 2011$) supports the paper, so this is a known disagreement between sources rather than an error.
- The 1992–9 Latvia/Lithuania ranking depends on source: MPD 2013 keeps Latvia above Lithuania 1993–9, World Bank 2005 PPP keeps Lithuania above throughout. The paper's claim "it had stood above it in the transition years 1992–9" is consistent with WB-based series but not with MPD.
- M&T p. 335 prints the Latvian declaration of renewed independence as 8 May 1990; the correct date (4 May 1990) is confirmed in norkus_2023_great p. 237. Do not cite M&T for the date.
- The OECD chapters (eight of ten) and the Meškauskas chapters contain only comparator tables; nothing in them supports a Latvian claim that is not better documented elsewhere. The one usable OECD table is the EU27=100 convergence table in oecd_2011_estonia p. 104.
- Norkus 2026 is an unpublished manuscript (its FSSR and Denmarkisation material duplicates the 2025 SEHR article, which should be cited instead); only its demographic passages (pp. 150, 152) add anything, and they in turn cite Klimantas 2025.
- Not checked: whether Latvia's "two and a half times the electricity it generated" (04_latvia) is consistent with M&T's 42 per cent energy import share for 1970 (different year and energy vs electricity).
