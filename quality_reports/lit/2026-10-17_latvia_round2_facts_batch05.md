## Facts by theme

### Pre-1914 level and the First World War

- In 1913 Latvia had 758 large industrial enterprises with 135,200 workers, among them the Russo-Balt wagon works and the rubber, linoleum and chemical giant Provodnik (16,000 employees in 1913); investing in Latvia and Estonia after 1945 was "cheaper and, in terms of pay-back, safer than in most 'old' regions of the USSR" because specialists, skilled workers and work culture survived. — vaskela_2013_lietuvos, pp. 115–116
- Riga handled 17.2 per cent of the Russian Empire's foreign trade in 1913, Ventspils 3.9, Liepāja 3.4 and Tallinn 10 per cent; by turnover Riga and Tallinn ranked first and third among Russian ports (St Petersburg 14.0 per cent). — vaskela_2013_lietuvos, p. 116
- Before 1914 Lithuania had 7.5 times fewer industrial workers than Latvia and produced 8.75 times less industrial output (2.5 and 2.6 times less than Estonia). — vaskelaistorija (existing key), p. 242
- "Overall the economy of our northern neighbours [Latvia and Estonia] was 'European', like northern Italy or southern Germany"; before 1914 industry was best developed in Latvia, even including Latgale, which was at the Lithuanian level. — vaskelaistorija (existing key), p. 327
- Russian seaborne trade through the Baltic ports collapsed after independence: Riga handled 23 (annual average 1911–13) against 9 in 1933, Windau and Libau 12 against 0.1, Reval 8 against 0.4 (table "Ein- und Ausfuhr in 1 000 t"; units as printed); Libau and Riga "remained essentially confined to their own national hinterland and lost their Russian catchment area" [verloren ihren russischen Einzugsbereich]. — willoweit_1969_wirtschaftsgeschichte, pp. 576–577
- Riga's population: 353,800 in 1940, 228,200 in 1945, 482,300 in 1950, 588,000 in 1960, 731,800 in 1970, 843,000 in 1980 and 909,135 in 1990 (Table 8.1; housing stock 6.8 mln m² in 1940, 16.4 mln in 1990). — treija_2019_socialist, p. 164

### Interwar reconstruction and the 1920s

- In 1922 the Latvian harvest of the three main grains was only 65 per cent of the pre-war level, against 91 per cent of 1913 for rye and barley in Estonia and 75 per cent for Soviet agriculture; Lithuania's exceeded pre-war by 30 per cent. — terleckaspinigai (existing key), p. 115
- In 1919 Lithuania lent 5 million marks to Latvia in return for the right to use the port of Liepāja (treaties of 28 February and 1 March 1919). — terleckas2000 (existing key), p. 8 (also terleckaspinigai, p. 63)
- Soviet Russia repaid the pre-war savings deposits of the Russian state banks neither to Lithuania nor to Latvia, Poland, Estonia or Finland. — terleckas2000 (existing key), p. 9
- Latvia established its emission bank on 19 September 1922, a week before the Bank of Lithuania (27 September 1922); the Latvian 1922 reform, like the Lithuanian and Czechoslovak, was carried out without League of Nations assistance. — terleckaspinigai (existing key), pp. 129, 166
- Latvian credit policy favoured agriculture until the end of 1926: up to 1 August 1929 two-thirds of all state credits went to agriculture and only 13 per cent to industry; under Social Democratic leadership and after the 1927 Latvian–Soviet trade treaty more emphasis was placed on industry. — valge_1994_tartu, p. 186
- Finland and Latvia "had adopted protectionist foreign trade policies and became among the greatest customs' citadels in Europe"; customs duties made up about half of Finland's state budget and one third of Latvia's, against under 15 per cent in Estonia. — valge_1994_tartu, pp. 186–187
- Both Estonia and Latvia "followed the credit pattern observed in Denmark up to the Great Depression". — valge_1994_tartu, p. 187
- After the 1927 Latvian–Soviet agreement "Latvian export to Soviet Russia, particularly of industrial products, increased many times over" and the Soviets left 40 million in transit taxes, "however, the Latvian enterprises that were built or expanded to fill Soviet orders often had difficulty finding other markets"; neither treaty gave "the impetus for a breakthrough in the European export market or for a rapid tempo of industrialization… Industrialization in both Estonia and Latvia was carried out to meet the needs of the home market." — valge_1994_tartu, p. 188
- Until the late 1920s Latvia and Estonia tried to preserve their pre-war industrial potential, "devoting the greater part of the budget to maintaining the industrial giants and reviving production that had collapsed during the war"; later they reoriented to the domestic market, accepting smaller plants and bankruptcies. — vaskelaistorija (existing key), p. 327
- Gold-and-foreign-exchange cover of the note issue c. 1926–31: Lithuania 51–63 per cent, Germany 58, France 55, Estonia 45, Latvia 32.7 per cent. — terleckaspinigai (existing key), p. 217
- Foreign debt per head in 1937: Lithuania 5.17 US dollars, Latvia 12.31, Estonia 27.70, Finland 12.97. — vaskelaistorija (existing key), p. 254
- Lithuanian import and export values per head were above Poland's "but lower than, for example, in Latvia and above all in Germany". — willoweit_1969_wirtschaftsgeschichte, pp. 679–680
- The Liepāja port, having lost Lithuanian goods, never regained its pre-war traffic: ship movements in 1924 were 54 per cent of 1911–13 (tonnage 57 per cent) and in 1937 31 per cent (tonnage 26 per cent), against 174 and 275 per cent at Klaipėda. — zostautaite_1990_klaipeda, p. 87
- In 1928–31 Latvia bought about 60 medium-sized ships; in 1939 Latvia had 102 merchant ships against Lithuania's 12 (12,257 GRT). — zostautaite_1990_klaipeda, p. 59; tijunelis_1998_ar, p. 134

### Agrarian reform

- In 1919 landlords owned 48.12 per cent of the territory of Latvia and 46.93 per cent of Estonia against 26.2 per cent in Lithuania (30.35 in Poland, 29 in Czechoslovakia); "in this respect Latvia and Estonia could be compared only with Hungary". Average estate 2,507 ha in Latvia, 2,113 in Estonia, 490 in Lithuania; average peasant farm in 1913 21.0 ha in Latvia, 29.4 in Estonia, 15.2 in Lithuania. — vaskela_1996_land, pp. 121–122 (same in vaskela_1998_zemes, pp. 56–58, 309; vaskelaistorija, p. 244)
- In 1905 landlords owned 48.3 per cent of the land in Latvia, about 60 per cent in Estonia and 36.4 per cent in Lithuania; in 1919 peasant farms held 39.32 per cent of the land in Latvia and 42.04 per cent in Estonia. — vaskela_1998_zemes, pp. 62, 66
- Latvia's Constituent Assembly passed the expropriation act on 16 September 1920 (distribution 21 December 1920, third part 3 May 1923); land was confiscated without compensation, landlords keeping 50 ha, and the referendum of 15 January 1924 rejected compensation (decree of 14 April 1924); Latvian citizens' farms of up to 100 ha, Latgalian folwarks, model and breeding farms were exempt, "in fact, even Latvian farms of over 100 ha were not confiscated". — vaskela_1996_land, p. 120 (= vaskela_1998_zemes, pp. 307–308)
- The state land fund covered 3,396,800 ha in Latvia (51.48 per cent of the territory), 2,375,000 ha in Estonia (49.95) and 1,591,400 ha in Lithuania (30.13); 84.09 per cent of landlord land was expropriated in Latvia (2,668,500 of 3,173,300 ha, 1,253 estates affected), 98.42 in Estonia, 76.68 in Lithuania. — vaskela_1996_land, pp. 126–127
- Compensation in Latvia was paid for 25 per cent of the confiscated area at 21–22 lats per ha, 10–12 times below the real value (a secret agreement of 12 February 1929 pledged payment for 250,000 ha taken from Polish landowners); by 1940 compensation had been paid for about 11 per cent of expropriated landlord land in Latvia, 25 per cent in Estonia and 50 per cent in Lithuania. — vaskela_1996_land, pp. 120, 127; vaskela_1998_zemes, pp. 88, 123
- Latvian new settlers paid an average 10 lats per ha (maximum 20), 20–27 times below market value; with Land Bank loans and taxes the burden was nearly 130 Ls/ha, half the 1937 market price of 200–270 Ls/ha, repayable over 41 years (60 years in Estonia, 36 in Lithuania); of the 181.1 million Ls due, 116.7 million had been paid by 1 January 1940. — vaskela_1996_land, p. 129; vaskela_1998_zemes, pp. 88, 124
- During the reform the number of private landowners rose by 39.3 per cent in Latvia (71.4 in Estonia, 18.4 in Lithuania); 36.9 per cent of Latvian peasants received additional land; peasant-owned land increased by 54.0 per cent in Latvia (69.3 in Estonia, 16.5 in Lithuania); farms under 20 ha increased 50.4 per cent in Latvia. New farms over 20 ha were 13.4 per cent of all new farms in Latvia, 24.9 in Estonia, 1.4 in Lithuania. — vaskela_1996_land, p. 129; vaskela_1998_zemes, pp. 152–155
- Peasants received 1,400,500 ha in Latvia, 1,220,700 in Estonia and 484,200 in Lithuania, i.e. 79, 86 and 38 per cent of the land that passed into farmers' ownership over 1919–40 (1.8, 1.4 and 1.3 million ha respectively); landlords in Latvia sold a further 454,500 ha of the 504,800 ha left to them. — vaskela_1998_zemes, p. 180
- Over 1919–40 landlord holdings shrank 62-fold in Latvia and 71-fold in Estonia (15-fold in Lithuania), from 47–48 to 0.6–0.8 per cent of the territory; state land rose from 10 to 27 per cent of Latvia's territory (2.7-fold). — vaskela_1998_zemes, p. 178
- Latvian land tenure on 22 July 1940 (Ministry of Agriculture project): state land 1,777,727 ha (26.94 per cent), private land 4,366,517 ha (66.18), church 18,177 ha, land of Germans repatriated to Germany 50,314 ha, urban land and infrastructure 385,084 ha; 4,435,008 ha belonged to 228,234 private owners. — vaskela_1998_zemes, pp. 174–175
- Latvia had 207,693 farms with 4,190,558 ha in 1929 and 224,854 farms with 4,517,114 ha in 1939 (census results published only thirty years later by Žagars); average peasant farm 20.18 ha (1929) and 20.09 ha (1939), against 23.2/22.7 in Estonia and 15.0/13.5 in Lithuania; farms of 1–20 ha were 67.01 per cent of Latvian farms in 1929 and 65.41 in 1939 (78–82 per cent in Lithuania). — vaskela_1998_zemes, p. 184
- Latvia held five agricultural censuses between the wars (Estonia three, Lithuania one, in 1930). — vaskela_1992_lietuvos, p. 26
- Agricultural labourers and semi-proletarian plot-holders were about 50 per cent of the rural population of Latvia around 1919–20 (about 100,000 labourer families, 42 per cent of the farming population), 55–60 per cent in Estonia and 25–27 per cent in Lithuania. — vaskela2005 (existing key), p. 480
- Hired labourers were 14.2 per cent (1929) to 18.0 per cent (1935) of the permanently employed agricultural population in Latvia, against 15.5 per cent in Lithuania (1930) and 15.2 in Estonia (1939); rural population density was 19.4 per km² in Latvia (1935), 37.5 in Lithuania (1939), 16.7 in Estonia (1934). — vaskela_1992_lietuvos, pp. 103, 105
- "The reform measures were most radically stated in Bulgaria and the countries of the Eastern Baltic region (excluding Finland). As to the relative quantities, the scope of the reform implementation was broadest in Latvia, Estonia and Lithuania"; the three reforms were "identical… the rates were the same". — vaskela_1996_land, pp. 131–132
- The Lithuanian government deliberately advertised its reform as "mild and humane" compared with the "semi-Bolshevik" reforms of Latvia and Estonia, although the pace was in fact the same. — vaskela2005 (existing key), p. 484; vaskelaistorija, p. 272
- In Lithuania, unlike Latvia, former owners were compensated with long-term state bonds. — vardys_1965_independent, p. 24

### Interwar growth, depression and the 1930s

- Lithuanian national income per head in 1924–34 was 60 per cent of Latvia's, 61 per cent of Estonia's and 54 per cent of Finland's (implying Latvia at about 90 per cent of Finland and roughly level with Estonia); the 2015 version gives 61, 63 and 58 per cent (Latvia ≈ 95 per cent of Finland). Average national income 1924–34 in million US dollars: Latvia 249, Lithuania 190, Estonia 142, Finland 473; population (thousands) 1,964, 2,525, 1,131, 3,602; gold reserves 9.2, 6, 8.6, 14.6 million dollars. — vaskela_1998_zemes, p. 289; vaskela_2013_lietuvos, p. 111; vaskelaistorija (existing key), p. 328
- Per-capita value of textile, chemical, metal-working and machinery output in 1939: Lithuania 5.8 US dollars, Latvia 22.8, Estonia (1938) 22.3, Finland 42.8; industrial employees 1935/36: Latvia 81,200, Estonia 41,800, Lithuania 25,700. — vaskela_2013_lietuvos, p. 111; vaskelaistorija, p. 328
- "Latvia and Estonia did not stand still either. Although somewhat behind Finland they restructured industry and adapted it to domestic needs; Latvia maintained its earlier volume of output, Estonia even increased it somewhat. With a falling production workforce, labour productivity in these states rose about twofold. Agricultural output grew one and a half times or more." Finnish industrial output grew 7.9 per cent a year in 1920–38 and GDP 4.7 per cent, "overtaking its southern neighbours Estonia and Latvia". — vaskelaistorija (existing key), p. 328
- Agriculture 1934–38: grain per head Latvia 580 kg (Lithuania 597, Estonia 447, Finland 396); grain yield 12.3 q/ha (11.7, 11.2, 15.6); potatoes 130.8 q/ha; milk per head 839 kg (506, 782, 715); per 100 inhabitants 62 cattle and 44 cows (Lithuania 46/30); per 100 ha arable 58 cattle, 40 cows, 78 t milk (Lithuania 43/28/47, Estonia 64/39/82, Finland 73/52/101); engine power 2.26 kW per 100 ha arable (Lithuania 1.28, Estonia 2.71); 23 engines per 1,000 farms in 1935 (Lithuania 14 in 1930, Estonia 28 in 1939). — vaskela_1998_zemes, pp. 290–294; vaskelaistorija, p. 329
- Arable land and gardens were 47.05 per cent of farm land in Latvia (1935) against 58.01 in Lithuania (1930) and 35.15 in Estonia (1939). — vaskelaistorija, p. 302; vaskela_1992_lietuvos, p. 103
- World butter exports 1938: 621,000 t, of which Denmark 158,000, Latvia 29,700 t (4.78 per cent), Lithuania 17,400 (2.80 per cent). — vaskelaistorija (existing key), p. 293; vaskela_1998_zemes, p. 277
- In the crisis years three joint-stock banks failed in Latvia and two large banks in Estonia, whereas no Lithuanian bank failed. — terleckaspinigai (existing key), p. 236
- Estonia left the gold parity in 1933 and Latvia in 1936, joining the sterling bloc. — terleckaspinigai (existing key), p. 222
- Note cover fell 28 per cent in Latvia and Poland in 1936–38 (Hungary 29, Switzerland 20, France and the Netherlands 18). — terleckaspinigai (existing key), p. 253
- The official discount rate was 4.5 per cent in Estonia, Latvia and Poland against 5–6 per cent in Lithuania and 1–2 per cent in the large states. — terleckaspinigai (existing key), p. 270
- Lithuania introduced exchange controls last in the Baltic (1935), after which Kaunas lost its name as "the financial centre of the Baltic states"; Lithuanian clearing agreements included Latvia. — terleckaspinigai (existing key), pp. 248, 250
- Between August and October 1939 money in circulation rose 47 per cent in Latvia, 31 in Estonia, 17 in Finland, 11 in Lithuania (6 in Britain, 17 in France, 22 in Germany). — terleckaspinigai (existing key), p. 269
- 1938 weekly wages of an industrial worker bought 1.9 pairs of shoes in Latvia, 1.7 in Lithuania, 1.2 in Poland and 0.35 in the Soviet Union; 56 lbs of meat in Latvia, 60 in Lithuania, 38 in Poland and 4.3 in the USSR; 0.55 of a woollen suit in Latvia, 0.33 in Lithuania, 0.09 in the USSR. — vardys_1965_independent, p. 28
- Cost-of-living comparison 1938–40 (minimum consumption basket, Lithuania = 1.00): Latvia 0.82, Estonia 0.68 (food 0.89/0.71, clothing 0.68/0.56, rent-fuel-light 0.80/0.71); the Latvian minimum rose 5.1 per cent in 1939 and 18.1 per cent by January–March 1940 (Lithuania 28.9). — vaskela_2007_darbo, pp. 26–27

- Real income per consumer unit of an employed worker's family in 1939: Latvia 116–121, Estonia 90 (Lithuania = 100); allowing for unemployment and part-time work, worker-family real income per consumer unit was 139–163 in Latvia and 108–124 in Estonia (Lithuania = 100): "urban workers' living standard in Lithuania was lower than in Estonia and in Latvia". — vaskela_2007_darbo, pp. 21–22, 29–30, 35
- Wage distribution: 51.5 per cent of Latvian industrial workers (June 1940) were in the low category and 0.09 per cent in the highest, against 52.5 and 6.8 per cent of Lithuanian sickness-fund members. — vaskela_2007_darbo, pp. 23, 34
- Worker-family budgets 1936–37: a worker's family member consumed 3,000 kg of food a year in Lithuania, 2,900 in Latvia, 2,800 in Estonia, with a calorie content above France, Italy, the USSR and the Balkans. — terleckaspinigai (existing key), p. 293
- Interwar Lithuanian democracy "did not lose, as it later did in Latvia, Estonia, and even Germany, as a direct result of strains imposed by the economic depression of the 1930's". — vardys_1965_independent, p. 35

### Sovietisation 1940–41 and the currency conversion

- A Soviet memorandum of 29 July 1940 set out three ways of introducing the rouble in the Baltic states; the first, "economically justified", was to raise the official rates of the national currencies to their purchasing-power level, which "would require raising the rate of the lat 6–8 times and the litas 5.4–7.2 times"; the second was two to three months of accelerated emission and inflation to raise Baltic prices to the Soviet level; the third, adopted, kept the "official (propaganda)" rouble rate. The Politburo decision of 14 August 1940 provided for parallel currencies and "the expediency of increasing inflation". — terleckaspinigai (existing key), pp. 290–291; terleckas_nd_astuntoji, pp. 11–12
- The Politburo decision of 2 October 1940 "On the emission of the local currencies of Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia" authorised additional emission by 15 November 1940 of 50 million litas, 89 million lats and 36 million kroons to cover budget deficits; in Latvia "it was planned to emit as much as 49 million lats more than its budget deficit", "so as to speed up the devaluation of the national currencies and extract more of the value created". — terleckaspinigai (existing key), pp. 293–294
- On 25 November 1940 the lat was equated with the rouble (the litas with 90 kopecks); "according to today's Latvian experts 1 lat was really worth 10 roubles, not one rouble"; Soviet secret documents of 1940 valued the lat at 6–8 roubles; "present-day Russian historians admit that the rouble's ratio to the Baltic currencies was set arbitrarily". — terleckas_nd_astuntoji, p. 14; terleckaspinigai, p. 296
- Retail prices in Lithuania were raised twice before the rouble (5 October and 23 November 1940, non-food goods 50–200 per cent, food 25–100 per cent); the second rise was postponed from 1 November because of "the enormous indignation of the people of the Baltic states"; Latvia had to introduce ration cards for sugar and household soap; a Russian historian holds that in October–November 1940 "the new power in the Baltic states was on the brink of crisis". — terleckas_nd_astuntoji, p. 12
- Deposit withdrawals were limited to 1,000 roubles in Lithuania; "in Latvia and Estonia, where saving was more developed, for some reason smaller sums, 100 and 125 roubles respectively, were allowed to be paid out". — terleckas2000 (existing key), p. 365
- Soviet Gosbank plenipotentiaries arrived in all three Baltic states by 24 July 1940; the rules of short-term crediting for the Baltic republics were confirmed on 20 February 1941; a secret USSR Sovnarkom decision of 5 June 1941 allowed Baltic residents to exchange state loan bonds up to 1,000 roubles. — terleckas2000 (existing key), pp. 361, 374–376
- The United States and Britain blocked the gold of the three Baltic central banks in July 1940; Soviet notes of 20 July and 26 August 1940 protested; Molotov told the US envoy on 26 September 1940 that the US and Britain had agreed not to recognise the Baltic incorporation or hand over the gold. — terleckaspinigai (existing key), pp. 284–286
- In Lithuania, "as in the other Baltic states, the owners of nationalised enterprises received no compensation", which is why Latvian historians use the term confiscation. — terleckas_nd_astuntoji, p. 8
- According to Latvian historians, full collectivisation was to begin in the summer of 1941. — terleckas_nd_astuntoji, p. 18

### German occupation 1941–44

- Under German occupation Reichskreditkassen marks (issued in Riga until mid-1942) circulated; the rouble was retained at a low rate only in Latvia, Estonia and the USSR, whereas the Dutch guilder was devalued 42 per cent, the Belgian franc 50 and the French franc 63. — terleckaspinigai, p. 300; terleckas_nd_astuntoji, p. 20
- Compulsory deliveries were introduced later in Estonia (1942); the 1942 milk quota for Latvian farmers is given as 27,000 t against 212–224 thousand t for Lithuanian, which the author finds "hard to believe". — terleckas_nd_astuntoji, p. 21
- In 1942 Generalkommissar Renteln reproached the Lithuanians that their farmers, with more land than the Latvians, paid half as much land tax (7.5 million RM against 15 million in Latvia). — terleckas_nd_astuntoji, p. 24

### Second World War population losses

- War losses in Latvia were "about 0.5–0.6 million inhabitants, mostly of Latvian nationality, who perished or fled to the West"; with a low natural increase (10,800 a year in 1949–58) there were 1.3 million Latvians in 1959, 170,000 fewer than before the war; with the arrival of almost 0.5 million East Slavs the Latvian share fell from 77 to 62 per cent; Estonians numbered about 0.9 million both in 1939 and 1959 and fell to 75 per cent. — truska_2000_kodel, p. 459
- Riga lost 35 per cent of its population between 1940 (353,800) and 1945 (228,200) and had regained 482,300 by 1950. — treija_2019_socialist, p. 164

### Post-war reconstruction, investment and Moscow's priorities (1945–60)

- Capital investment (excluding kolkhozes, comparable prices) 1946–60: Latvia 1,834 million roubles (312 in 1946–50, 501 in 1951–55, 1,016 in 1956–60), Estonia 1,540 (301/474/765), Lithuania 1,559 (182/394/983); Latvia received 37.18 per cent of the Baltic total, 39.25 per cent in 1946–50; the Baltic share of all-Union investment was only 2.19 per cent in 1946–60 (2.29, 2.04, 2.24), "less than the Eastern Baltic's share of production", so "there is no need to speak of any additional investment from Moscow". — vaskela_2014_lietuvos, pp. 232–233, 242–243; vaskela_2013_lietuvos, p. 110
- Kolkhoz investment 1946–60: Latvia 275 million roubles (40.15 per cent of the Baltic total), Lithuania 262, Estonia 148. — vaskela_2014_lietuvos, p. 243
- Per head of population the Eastern Baltic republics "were indeed invested in more than most other regions of the USSR or of Russia itself", but "priority financing… is linked not with altruistic motives but with concrete political and economic aims". — vaskela_2013_lietuvos, p. 110
- Moscow's post-war tasks in the Baltic were German reparations transport (Latvian and Estonian ports), gas for Leningrad (Estonian shale) and transport reconstruction (the Riga wagon works and other large Latvian and Estonian plants); Lithuania had at best a secondary role. — vaskela_2014_lietuvos, pp. 227–228
- In Latvia the Riga wagon, foundry, mechanical, cement and glass works resumed production in 1945; VEF, the Daugavpils locomotive repair works and the Riga superphosphate plant were priorities; the fifth five-year plan (1951–55) included the Riga thermal power station (129.5 MW); "strategically important objects for the whole USSR began operating or were started in Latvia and Estonia from 1945", unlike Lithuania, which became strategically significant only from the late 1960s. — vaskela_2013_lietuvos, pp. 115, 117
- Latvia and Estonia were oriented by Moscow to the needs of Leningrad (North-West economic region), Lithuania to Moscow's (Centre region); the three republics formed the "Western" economic region in the statistical compendia of the late 1950s. — vaskela_2014_lietuvos, pp. 226–227
- "Sniečkus is credited for convincing Stalin to overlook Lithuania while pushing for a quick industrial development in Estonia and Latvia. Lithuania's Baltic neighbors were also likely preferred by Stalin, however, because they already possessed an industrial infrastructure and some well-developed industries." Lithuania's industrialisation "did not seriously begin until Stalin's death". — vardys_1997_lithuania, p. 66
- Industrial workers in Latvia: 84,800 in 1940, 54,800 in 1945, 135,800 in 1950, 174,500 in 1955, 233,400 in 1960 (Lithuania 45.5/38.3/76.6/128.0/178.7 thousand; Estonia 61.2/45.4/86.7/115.0/137.2); Latvia's share of Baltic industrial workers 44.3 per cent (1940), 45.4 (1950), 42.5 (1960); the Baltic share of USSR industrial workers rose from 1.96 per cent (1945) to 3.00 (1960). (The 2013 article prints 35.8 for Latvia 1950, evidently a misprint for 135.8.) — vaskela_2014_lietuvos, p. 244; vaskela_2013_lietuvos, p. 111
- Gross industrial output (1975 prices): Latvia 0.5 bn roubles in 1950, 1.0 in 1955, 1.9 in 1960 (Lithuania 0.3/0.8/1.6, Estonia 0.3/0.7/1.2); Latvia's share of Baltic industrial output fell from 45.5 per cent (1950) to 40.0 (1955) and 40.4 (1960) while Lithuania's rose from 27.3 to 34.0; the Baltic share of USSR industrial output rose from 2.1 to 3.0 per cent. — vaskela_2014_lietuvos, pp. 233, 245
- Physical output of Latvia 1945/1955/1959: electricity 81.7/928.4/1,486.2 mln kWh; cement 16.2/369.2/415.2 thousand t; cotton fabrics 3.0/46.5/49.0 mln m; woollen fabrics 446/6,646/8,134 thousand m; leather footwear 398/4,097/6,773 thousand pairs; paper 4.8/57.3/75.9 thousand t; industrial meat 9.3/28.1/70.8 thousand t; fish catch 55.4 (1955), 104.6 (1959) thousand t. — vaskela_2014_lietuvos, pp. 238–239
- National income (current prices): Latvia 1,306.9 million roubles in 1956, 1,423.5 in 1957, 1,595.2 in 1958, 1,667.9 in 1959, 1,749.0 in 1960 (Lithuania 1,122.0 → 1,796.2; Estonia 743.4 → 984.8); Lithuania, with a population a third larger, lagged behind Latvia in 1956–58 and only just exceeded it in 1959–60. — vaskela_2014_lietuvos, pp. 232, 242
- Official Soviet statistics claimed that industrial output regained the pre-war level in Estonia in 1946 and in Latvia in 1947, which "many researchers in Latvia and Estonia dispute"; Mertelsmann argues that the Soviet statistics understated independent Estonia's output through a rouble–kroon rate of 1:1.25, that the rouble was worth far less in 1955 than in 1940, and that pre-war output was regained only in 1955. — vaskela_2014_lietuvos, p. 229
- According to Soviet statistics, industrial output in 1959 was 9.1 times the 1940 level in Lithuania against 4.8 times in the USSR, 3.8 in Belorussia, 3.4 in Ukraine and 4.5 in Russia; "the only republic whose rate of industrial growth approached that of the Baltic republics was Moldavia" (8.3 times); such comparisons are suspect because the 1940 base covers only the months after annexation. — zunde_1965_lithuania, p. 156
- By 1990 industry produced 60.3 per cent of Latvia's national product and 61.7 per cent of Estonia's, against 54 per cent in Lithuania. — vardys_1997_lithuania, p. 66

### Collectivisation and agriculture 1945–59

- After the war Baltic agriculture "was on the verge of collapse": in 1946 more than 100,000 ha, almost 30 per cent of all arable land, was left unsown in Latvia (citing Zubkova). — terleckas_2026_siferio, p. 193
- The Central Committee decided on 21 May 1947 to found kolkhozes in Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia; by the end of 1947 Latvia had 46 or 30 kolkhozes (by different data), Lithuania 20, Estonia 5; Arvīds Pelše told the Latvian party plenum in October 1948 that collectivisation "will not go on for 15–20 years" but implied it would not be abrupt. — terleckas_2026_siferio, pp. 219–221
- The May 1948 deportation of about 40,000 people took place only in Lithuania; "in Latvia and Estonia an analogous operation, unlike in 1949, did not take place". — terleckas_2026_siferio, p. 222
- "In Estonia and Latvia, where interwar farming was larger-scale, collectivisation went faster: on 1 January 1950 collectivisation in Lithuania had reached 62.4 per cent, in Estonia 80 per cent, in Latvia 90 per cent." — terleckas_2026_siferio, p. 225
- "In Latvia and Estonia there were no mass deportations in 1951, because collectivisation was already complete." — terleckas_2026_siferio, p. 230
- Latvian agricultural output 1945/1955/1959: grain 684/328/611 thousand t; potatoes 675/1,124/2,003; sugar beet 88/138/118; milk 716/981/1,440 thousand t; meat (carcass) 50/101/140; eggs 63/202/301 million; cattle 678/808/843 thousand; pigs 336/600/751 thousand. The Baltic share of USSR grain fell from 5.10 per cent in 1945 to 0.92 in 1955; "by the early 1950s Stalin's agrarian policy had run Baltic agriculture completely into the ground" [visiškai nuvariusi]. — vaskela_2014_lietuvos, pp. 230–231, 240–241
- At a conference of Baltic agricultural experts in Riga in June 1955 Khrushchev declared that the amalgamation of collective farms in the Baltic "had been carried on with too much fervor". — zunde_1965_lithuania, p. 152

### 1950–68: growth, immigration, sovnarkhoz

- Moscow's aim in industrialising the Baltic republics was political and strategic; giant plants were built to serve all three republics, e.g. the Vievis (Elektrėnai) power station (1.2 million kW) to supply Latvia, Kaliningrad and Belorussia, and the Kėdainiai chemical plant to supply fertiliser to the Baltic republics. — zunde_1965_lithuania, pp. 156, 161–162
- Urban population in 1959: Latvia 56 per cent, Estonia 56, Lithuania 39 (Pskov region 27, Vitebsk 32); "the labor situation in both of the other Baltic republics, Latvia and Estonia, is much the same or even worse than in Lithuania", so the planned industrialisation could only be manned by migration from Russia. — zunde_1965_lithuania, pp. 167–168
- Natural increase 1959–88 totalled 246,000 in Latvia, 165,000 in Estonia and 804,000 in Lithuania; per 1,000 inhabitants it fell in Latvia from 5.5 (1959–65) to 1.3 (1976–80); in the late 1970s–early 1980s Latvian deaths exceeded births; ethnic Latvians grew by only 90,000 (6.0 per cent) between 1959 and 1989 (1,298,000 to 1,388,000) against a 36 per cent rise of Lithuanians. — truska_2000_kodel, p. 460
- In the 1989 census the titular nationality was 52.0 per cent of the population in Latvia, 61.5 in Estonia and 79.6 in Lithuania; Latvians were 36 per cent of Riga's population in 1989 against over 63 per cent in 1935; Riga, with 910,000 inhabitants in the late 1980s, held more than a third of the republic's population; Latvians were a majority in only two of the ten largest Latvian towns in 1989 (13 per cent in Daugavpils, 39 in Liepāja, 43 in Ventspils). — truska_2000_kodel, pp. 457, 463
- Soviet industrialisation in Latvia and Estonia was concentrated in one centre (Riga, Tallinn), whereas Lithuania built new industrial towns staffed by locals (only 3–5 per cent of workers in them came from the USSR). — truska_2000_kodel, p. 463
- Lithuania's industry, "previously unable even remotely to compare with its industrial neighbours Latvia and Estonia, in the 1970s essentially caught up with them"; after the abolition of the sovnarkhozes in 1965 "the economy of Lithuania and the other Baltic republics was subordinated exclusively to all-Union interests". — vaskela_2013_lietuvos, pp. 111, 113
- The Khrushchev interlude of 1957–64 was "the only interlude" in centralised management; Andropov and Gorbachev treated the integrated economy as the cornerstone of the Soviet state. — vardys_1997_lithuania, p. 66
- Soviet housing programme: the 1957 resolution set the goal of a separate apartment for each family by 1980; about 70 per cent of Riga's housing was built by prefabricated methods; from 1965 Latvian architects could adapt USSR standard projects; the second large-slab plant (1972) had a capacity of 240,000 m² a year. — treija_2019_socialist, pp. 163, 171–172
- About 200,000 apartments were built in Riga in 1958–90, construction peaking in 1987, but demand was not met: useful living space per person in Riga was 19.2 m² in 1940 and only 18 m² at the beginning of the 1990s (12.5 m² in 1960). — treija_2019_socialist, pp. 164, 174

### 1968–89: stagnation, investment and relative income

- 1988 capital stock per head: Latvia 114.0 (USSR = 100), Estonia 125.0, Lithuania 110.1, Russia 119.0; 1988 investment flow per head: Latvia 95.1, Estonia 106.2, Lithuania 112.8, Russia 122.8; Latvia held 1 per cent of the Union's capital stock and 0.9 per cent of its 1988 investment. — vanselm_1997_economics, p. 35
- 1988 GNP per head: Latvia 124.2 (USSR = 100), Estonia 121.6, Belarus 116.6, Russia 115.8, Lithuania 109.9; GNP per unit of capital stock Latvia 109.0, Estonia 97.2, Lithuania 99.9. — vanselm_1997_economics, p. 35
- In 1989 Gosplan chairman Maslyukov put Lithuanian per-capita income at 2,757.95 roubles, "somewhat below Estonia and Latvia, but 9 per cent above the Soviet average", and Lithuanian wages 2 per cent above the Soviet average "after Estonia, Latvia, and Russia"; Moscow claimed 50 billion roubles of investment in Lithuania (Gorbachev: 30 billion), which the Lithuanian statisticians countered came largely from local kolkhoz funds and savings, while ministry profits were not passed back. — vardys_1997_lithuania, p. 68
- "Pollution of the Baltic Sea, heavier on the more industrial Latvian coast…". — vardys_1997_lithuania, p. 70
- Lithuania, unlike Latvia and Estonia, avoided many ecological problems and heavy immigration from the East; Lithuanian development in 1945–60 was based more on its own resources and needs. — vaskela_2013_lietuvos, p. 123
- Latvia declared sovereignty on 28 July 1989 and independence on 4 May 1990; the draft Union Treaty of 20 August 1991 would have allowed the Baltic republics to secede; they did not join the CIS. — vanselm_1997_economics, pp. 29–31

### Transition 1990–95 and the 1990s

- "In the first 5–6 years of reform, GDP in Latvia, Lithuania and Estonia fell by 51%, 44% and 35% respectively"; average annual growth 1990–2000 was −3.4 per cent in Latvia, −2.3 in Lithuania and −0.5 in Estonia; in the years before accession average growth was 7.5 per cent in Latvia, 6.7 in Lithuania, 6.5 in Estonia. — tsaurkubule_2024_socio, p. 154
- Baltic GDP contracted by 9.6 per cent a year on average in 1990–95 and grew by 6.54 per cent a year after 1995, which "could not be compensated"; growth was interrupted in 1999 by the Russian crisis; 2000–07 the Baltic economies had the highest growth rates in Europe. — yucel_2014_fdi, pp. 119–120
- Transformation "proceeded fast on very liberal foundations in Estonia, followed later by Latvia and Lithuania"; only Estonia was a first-tier accession country until the Helsinki Council of December 1999. — yucel_2014_fdi, p. 118
- Latvian privatisation used vouchers and "led to the concentration of the most valuable assets in the hands of a small elite", whereas Estonia privatised for money without vouchers and liquidated unprofitable enterprises quickly. — tsaurkubule_2024_socio, p. 155
- Latvia and Estonia "began selling off their resources and industries, while Lithuania took measures to preserve its existing production systems. As a result, Lithuania continues to trade in industrial products, while Latvia has the largest negative trade balance". — tsaurkubule_2024_socio, p. 162
- Free-market food prices on 14 January 1992 (roubles/kg): beef fillet Riga 80, Tallinn 120, St Petersburg 320, Moscow 190; butter Riga 140, Tallinn 100, Vilnius 80; eggs (10) Riga 17, Tallinn 25; potatoes Riga 8, Tallinn 14, Vilnius 5. — wbtransitionest (existing key), p. 106
- Transit rail traffic between Riga and north-west Russia through Valga fell from 15 to 3 freight trains a day in 1992; the north-west grid was dispatched from Riga; in 1990 Estonia exported 7.0 TWh, 48.5 per cent of it to Latvia, and in 1991 3.2 TWh to Latvia. — wbtransitionest (existing key), pp. 130, 135, 139
- Latvia's Minimum Income Guarantee spending was 0.02–0.05 per cent of GDP in 1999–2004 (Estonia 0.17–0.41): "Latvia's welfare state is particularly lean and mean". — vanhuysse_2009_power, p. 61
- Titular Latvians were 54 per cent of the population (under half of the 19–44 age group) but 79 per cent of voters in 1993; only 39 per cent of ethnic Russians could vote; non-citizens were denied the vote even in local elections until 1994. — vanhuysse_2009_power, p. 63
- Westward exit of Baltic workers rich in human capital was "diluting the human capital base of Baltic political economies, driving them further along their distinctly low-skill, low-quality, low-value-added export production pathway". — vanhuysse_2009_power, p. 63
- Almost 99 per cent of municipal and state housing was offered for privatisation from 1991; privately owned apartments rose from 1.2 per cent (1996) to 68 (2000) and 85 per cent (2006). — treija_2019_socialist, p. 174

### 1995–2024: boom, crash and divergence

- GDP growth 1995–2002: Latvia 42.6 per cent, Estonia 40.5, Lithuania 31.9 (citing Tiusanen). — verkuleviciute_2014_change, p. 191
- GDP per capita in 2004: Estonia 55 per cent of the EU average, Latvia 44, Lithuania 50; in 2012 Estonia and Lithuania above 70 per cent, Latvia 64; Baltic GDP per capita rose 46 per cent on average in 2004–13; Latvia's GDP per capita relative to Estonia (= 100): 78.0 in 1995, 65.0 in 2004, 86.8 in 2008, 84.0 in 2013; Lithuania and Latvia "by GDP per capita almost equal" in 2013. — verkuleviciute_2014_change, pp. 191, 196
- In 2009 GDP shrank by more than 10 per cent in all three; the lowest pre-crisis unemployment was 6.2 per cent in Latvia (2007) against 4.2–4.6 in Lithuania and Estonia; Latvian unemployment reached 19.8 per cent in 2010. — verkuleviciute_2014_change, pp. 191, 194–196
- "Of all the countries in transition region, Latvia has been one of the most severely affected by the global economic crisis. GDP growth decelerated sharply from 10 percent in 2007 to −4.6 percent in 2008", driven by collapsing credit growth and asset prices; FDI in Latvia fell markedly in 2001 after the 1998 Russian crisis. — yucel_2014_fdi, pp. 122–123
- EU funds 2007–13: Latvia's allocation was 66.3 per cent of Lithuania's; interim payments by end-2013 were 67 per cent for Lithuania and Estonia but only 55.8 per cent for Latvia. — verkuleviciute_2014_change, p. 190
- Wages since accession rose 51–52 per cent in Estonia and Lithuania and 44 per cent in Latvia; in Latvia "wage growth outpaces the increase in productivity" because of labour shortage; consumer prices rose 47.9 per cent in Latvia 2005–12 against 39–40 in Estonia and Lithuania. — verkuleviciute_2014_change, pp. 192–193
- Latvia (with Portugal and Lithuania) had the highest income inequality in the EU in 2006; in 2011 Latvia's Gini was above Lithuania's. — verkuleviciute_2014_change, pp. 192–193
- Household consumption per capita 2012: Estonia 284.6 EUR a month, Latvia 282, Lithuania 247.3. — verkuleviciute_2014_change, p. 193
- GDP per capita in PPS as a percentage of the EU average, 2020–23: Latvia 69, 71, 69, 70; Lithuania 87, 88, 88, 87; Estonia 85, 85, 84, 80. Real GDP growth 2020–23: Latvia −3.5, 6.9, 1.8, 2.9; Lithuania 0.0, 6.4, 2.5, 0.3; Estonia −2.9, 7.2, 0.1, −3.0. Population (thousands): Latvia 1,900.5 (2020) to 1,877.5 (2023); Lithuania 2,810.4 to 2,871.6; Estonia 1,329.5 to 1,370.3. Emigration (thousands): Latvia 12.0, 13.0, 16.7, 16.3; Lithuania 25.2, 25.2, 15.3, 22.0. Government debt 2023: Latvia 43.6 per cent of GDP, Lithuania 38.3, Estonia 19.6; shadow economy: Latvia 22.9, Lithuania 26.4, Estonia 17.9 per cent of GDP. Average monthly wage 2023: Latvia 1,537 EUR, Lithuania 2,022, Estonia 1,832. — tsaurkubule_2024_socio, pp. 158–159 (Tables 2–3)
- Agriculture is 4.4 per cent of GDP and 6.8 per cent of employment in Latvia (2022) against 2.2/2.6 in Estonia and 3.0/5.5 in Lithuania; Latvia's arable land is about 2 million ha; after accession Latvia "completely dismantled its once well-developed sugar industry" and the food, textile and pharmaceutical sectors suffered. — tsaurkubule_2024_socio, pp. 156–157
- "The transformation processes of the transition to a market economy led to a deep economic recession – in 1991–1994 there was a sharp decline in GDP, income of the population and hyperinflation." Russia assumed all Soviet debts, so the Baltic states started "with their public debt effectively reduced to zero". — tsaurkubule_2024_socio, p. 154

### Soviet statistical practice (relevant to Section 3)

- Soviet comparable-price statistics ignored world prices, allowed manipulation through the price differences between grades of the same product, and valued later output at what it "could have cost earlier", so that "the method of calculating general indicators of economic development applied in communist Russia was characterised by a tendency to inflate the indicators of the later period"; aggregate indicators for Lithuania were neither calculated nor published before 1957. — vaskela_2014_lietuvos, pp. 228–229
- The first post-war census in the Baltic republics was taken only in January 1959; post-war statistical data are scarce. — truska_2000_kodel, p. 458
- "There may be grounds for doubting the credibility of Soviet statistics, particularly in the area of industry… they are based not on the entire industrial output for 1940, but on production computed beginning with the annexation." — zunde_1965_lithuania, p. 156
- The much-praised Estonian economic statistics began only in 1923; for 1920–22 "it was easy for businessmen to conceal their income". — valge_1994_tartu, p. 186

## Gaps and caveats

- Page numbering of `truska_2000_kodel` (pp. 457–463) is that of the extraction and looks like a reprint pagination rather than the original *Akiračiai* issue; verify before citing.
- `terleckas_nd_astuntoji` is an undated web essay; the "pages" are printout pages. Its Latvian figures (lat worth 10 roubles; 1942 milk quota 27,000 t) are second-hand and the author himself doubts the milk figure.
- The World Bank Estonia and Lithuania chapters are reused under the existing keys `wbtransitionest` / `wbtransitionlith` (whole-volume entries); the existing `worldbank_1993_*` techreport keys refer to the Latvia volume, not these chapters.
- `vaskela_2013_lietuvos` prints 35.8 thousand industrial workers for Latvia in 1950; the fuller 2014 table gives 135.8, which is consistent with the Baltic total (299.1). Treat 35.8 as a misprint.
- Vaškela's 1946–60 investment figures (comparable prices, excluding kolkhozes) sit uneasily with his statement (p. 117 of the 2013 article) that Estonia was allocated 3.5 billion roubles in 1946–50; the latter is in a different price basis (pre-1961 roubles) and should not be mixed with the 301 million in the table.
- Willoweit's port table (p. 577) is headed "in 1 000 t" but the magnitudes (Riga 23 in 1911–13) suggest a larger unit; cite only the collapse ratios, not the levels.
- The Vaškela 1996 article and the 1998 English summary (pp. 304–319) are the same text; the Lithuanian chapters of the 1998 monograph add the Latvian land-tenure tables (pp. 174–175, 181, 184–186) and allotment data (pp. 146–157). One `@book` entry (`vaskela_1998_zemes`) covers all seven chapter folders; cite chapter pages.
- Van Selm's tables for 1988 (pp. 35) are compiled from Goskomstat 1989 and Burkett 1992 and carry OCR damage in some rows (not in the Latvian ones); GNP per capita there is a Soviet-statistics construct, not a PPP measure.
- Tsaurkubule and Zhukovskaya's 1990s growth figures and the "51 per cent" transition fall come from the national statistical portals (total GDP, official series), not per-capita PPP estimates; the 2020–23 indicator table (p. 159) is the most useful part and is sourced to Eurostat and the national offices.
- No source in this batch bears on the 1941 June deportation count, the 1962–63 agricultural crisis in Latvia, the 1959 purge, Latvia's 1970–85 industrial growth ranking, the 1974–82 harvest depression or Bolotin's 1990 benchmark.
- Lithuanian-language sources were translated by the reviewer; original wording is bracketed where it matters.
