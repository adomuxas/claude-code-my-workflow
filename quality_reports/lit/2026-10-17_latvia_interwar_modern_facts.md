## 1. Interwar Latvia (1918–1940)

### 1.1 Pre-1914 baseline and WWI losses
- Pre-war Latvia had c. 2.5 million inhabitants (1914) vs 1,596,000 at the start of 1920; Riga fell from 520,000 (1914) to 225,000 (Dec 1920) because industry and officials were evacuated to inner Russia from 1915 — Karnups 2017, p. 103 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_valsts_tautsaimniecibas_in_krumins_latvijas_tautsaimniecibas_vesture]. Mežs argues the 2.55 m figure is inflated and the true 1914 population was 2.25–2.30 m, so the WWI loss "did not exceed 0.7 million" but still "approached 30%" — Mežs 2024, p. 185 [mezs_2024_latvijas_iedzivotaji_esosie_in_krumins_turbulence_virzitajspeks_latvijas].
- Evacuation removed 500 industrial enterprises and resettled 200,000 skilled workers; Riga's population fell from 472,068 (1913) to 166,536 (1920) — Wezel 2025, p. 131 [wezel_2025_latvia_between_economic_in_richter_et_al_great_depression_eastern]. 1913: 1,251 enterprises with 137,539 workers; 1920: 1,430 enterprises with 21,213 workers, "only 23% of the 1910 number"; machinery promised in the 1920 peace treaty was never returned — Karnups 2017, p. 112 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. Norkus: only 1% of evacuated equipment returned; on 7% of the territory more than half of buildings were destroyed — Norkus 2023, pp. 136–137 [norkus_2023_post_communist_transformations_ch_output_growth_baltic].
- Pre-war Riga: 3rd industrial city of the Empire; Latvia with 1.5% of Russia's population produced almost 6% of its industrial output; industrial output per head 120 rubles vs 40 in Russia; "in value of industrial output Latvia was comparable to Finland" — Krūmiņš & Šiliņš 2017, p. 42 [krumins_silins_2017_latvijas_tautsaimnieciba_gadsimta_in_krumins_latvijas_tautsaimniecibas_vesture]. 28.2% of Russian exports passed through Latvian ports in 1913; contemporaries saw Latvia as the "Belgium of the East" (Hiden & Salmon) — Norkus 2023, p. 135 [norkus_2023_…_output_growth_baltic].
- "In various post-war calculations Latvia was recognised as the country in the world that suffered most from the war" — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 44 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_turbulence_gadsimta_in_krumins_turbulence_virzitajspeks_latvijas]. "The population had decreased by 28%, industrial output had declined by 83%" — Lyons & Morrison 2001, PDF p. 2 [lyons_morrison_2001_latvia_history_in_bell_central_south_eastern].
- Re-agrarianisation: agriculture's share of employment 45% (1897), 80% (1920), 66% (1930); industry 20%, 7%, 14% — Karnups 2017, p. 104 (Table 1) [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. "Industry ... was evacuated to the Russian interior. Neither Riga nor Latvia would recapture an industrial character until ... forcibly incorporated into a Soviet political entity" — Purs 2012, p. 44 [purs_2012_baltic_facades_ch_historical_background].
- State finances 18 Nov 1918–1 Apr 1920: revenue 64.8 m lats-equivalent, of which paper-money emission 37.9 m (almost 60%) — Jēkabsons 2017, p. 77 [jekabsons_2017_latvijas_saimnieciskas_dzives_in_krumins_latvijas_tautsaimniecibas_vesture]. 2.52 bn Latvian rubles issued by March 1921; inflation halted mid-1921 — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 253 [krumins_2024_naudas_parsteigumi_in_krumins_turbulence_virzitajspeks_latvijas].

### 1.2 The 1920 agrarian reform
- Law of 16 Sept 1920; State Land Fund took 61% of all land and 45% of agricultural land; former owners kept c. 50 ha; 54,243 new farms averaging 17.1 ha; 1929: 216,300 farms, 68% under 20 ha — Karnups 2017, pp. 105–107 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. No compensation except a secret 5.3 m lats payment to Poland (1929–37) — Krūmiņš 2017, p. 23 [krumins_2017_laika_posma_raksturojums_lidz_1940_…]; Krūmiņš 2024, p. 242 [krumins_2024_naudas_…].
- Large landlords owned 48.12% of land in Latvia, 46.93% in Estonia, 26.20% in Lithuania (1919) — Norkus 2023, p. 137 [norkus_2023_…_output_growth_baltic]. Reform "promoted the transition to intensive agriculture on the Danish model" [pāreju uz intensīvo lauksaimniecību pēc Dānijas parauga]; agriculture regained pre-war level in 1929 — Karnups 2017, p. 132 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. Krūmiņš calls it "the best and strategically most correct political decision"; 1935: 740,000 people were landowners or family members — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 52, 76 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…].
- Aizsilnieks's critique (via Krūmiņš): small farms tied up under-employed labour; net output per industrial worker (1934 prices) fell from 2,300 lats (1934) to 1,800 (1938) — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 67, 77 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…].

### 1.3 Currency: the lats (1922) and the Bank of Latvia
- Latvian ruble sole legal tender 18 March 1920; lats introduced 3 Aug 1922 at 0.2903226 g gold (Swiss franc parity); Bank of Latvia law 7 Sept 1922, 10 m lats capital, 100% cover policy; "Latvia had no difficulty holding this rate until 1931" — Karnups 2017, pp. 124–126 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. Reserves c. 95 m lats against 40–45 m lats of notes in 1928 (200% cover) — p. 129.
- Money in circulation 1925 only 45.49 lats per capita vs Sweden 59.53, Denmark 75.68, UK 100.90: monetary policy "cautious and conservative" — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 256–257 (Table 4.2) [krumins_2024_naudas_…].
- The 18 March 1920 conversion law valued the Russian ruble about 200 times below its pre-war value; depositors received "0.48% of the gold value" — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 236, 238 [krumins_2024_naudas_…].

### 1.4 1920s recovery, foreign capital, trade policy
- Industrial output 135.7 m Ls (1922) → 344.2 m (1927); exports grew "almost 10-fold" 1921–29; trade balance negative every year to 1929 — Karnups 2017, pp. 116, 120–121 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. Latvian industry exported only 11.7% of output by 1927 (74% in 1914); gross industrial output before 1929 only 47% of 1913 — Norkus 2023, p. 137 [norkus_2023_…_output_growth_baltic].
- Foreign capital 53.5% of joint-stock capital in 1929 (94.5 m Ls); >60% of bank share capital by 1927 — Karnups 2017, pp. 113–114 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. "Access to capital emerged as the Achilles heel" — Purs 2012, p. 123 [purs_2012_baltic_facades_ch_economic_developments].
- Soviet trade treaty 2 June 1927: exports to USSR 3.8 m Ls (1927) → 40.1 m (1929) → 14.2 m (1932); 82% industrial goods — Karnups 2017, pp. 114–115 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. Transit was "negligible", significant only during NEP 1921–26 — p. 123.
- Butter exports 15.2 t (1921) → 14,830 t (1929, 21.5% of exports); 450 cooperative dairies 1929; timber c. 30% of exports, 65% to Britain; flax fell from 24.8% to 5.0% of exports — Karnups 2017, pp. 109–111 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. 1921–29: Germany 41.8% of imports, Britain 35.4% of exports — p. 121.
- Tariff 1921/22 >20% of import value; 1928 max/min tariff law; MFN treaties with all major states by 1929; Latvian–Estonian customs union (1923, 1927) never realised — Karnups 2017, pp. 116–120 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…].

### 1.5 The Great Depression in Latvia (Wezel 2025 and others)
- Timing: the crisis reached Latvia only in Q4 1930 (Soviet treaty cushion) — Karnups 2017, p. 126 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. Timber already in recession before Wall Street: A. E. Grube bankruptcy 20 Oct 1929 from Soviet dumping — Wezel 2025, pp. 141–142 [wezel_2025_…]. 1930 trade only 13% below 1929; 1932 "the nadir": seaborne trade 70.5% below 1929 and below 1922 — Wezel 2025, p. 144.
- Depth: imports/exports 362.1/273.9 m Ls (1929) → 84.6/96.5 (1932) — Karnups 2017, p. 128 (Table 13) [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. National income c. 1,151 m Ls (1929/30, 600 Ls per head) → 744 m (1932, 390 Ls), −35% nominal; Ceichners: real fall "at most some 10–12%" — p. 130. Riga sawmills 20 (1927) → 6 (1932); timber workforce 9,131 → 5,404 — Wezel 2025, p. 145. Registered urban unemployment peaked at 39,086 (Jan 1932) — Karnups 2017, p. 131. Butter price 3.67–4.04 Ls/kg (Sept 1929) → 2.86–2.96 (May 1930); butter exports 58.7 m Ls (1929) → 14.3 m (1934) — Wezel 2025, pp. 147–148.
- "A common, yet incorrect, view holds that the depression was not as severe in Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania as elsewhere. On the contrary, bread lines, debtors' auctions and mass unemployment existed" — Purs 2012, p. 123 [purs_2012_…_economic_developments]. Contrast: "this crisis did not hit Latvia as hard as other European countries" — Karnups 2017, p. 133.
- Banking 1931: Bank of Latvia restricted credit from 1 Jan 1931; bankruptcies peaked 1932; exchange control imposed 8 Oct 1931, black-market premium up to 35% — Karnups 2017, pp. 128–129. Wezel describes "unexpectedly high credit restrictions by local banks", a Currency Board (Valūtas Kommisija) at the Finance Ministry licensing all imports and foreign payments, but gives no named bank failure — Wezel 2025, pp. 146–148. Purs: "Runs on banks, although not commonplace, existed, and the private banking industry suffered considerably" — Purs 2012, p. 123. Foreign-currency share of reserves fell to 10.5% by 1934; wholesale index 120 (1929) → 83 (1934); exporters received only 47% of 1929 lats value — Karnups 2017, pp. 129–130.
- Devaluation: 28 Sept 1936, "as almost the last state in Europe", two days after France left gold; sterling from Ls 15.58 to 25.22; foreign-currency debts "overnight almost doubled" — Wezel 2025, p. 148. Official gold value −39%; £1 = 25.22 Ls restores the pre-1931 rate — Karnups 2017, p. 146 [karnups_2017_karla_ulmana_autoritara_…]. Karnups: not devaluing with Britain in 1931 "was, in the author's opinion, a mistake" [pēc autora domām, bija kļūda] — Karnups 2017, p. 133 [karnups_2017_neatkarigas_…]. Krūmiņš: delay "decided only in 1936, strongly belated" [vien 1936. gadā, stipri novēloti] — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 59 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…]. Norkus et al.: "devaluations were delayed, but this was a formality after foreign currency controls were introduced" — Norkus et al. 2025, p. 17 [norkus_et_al_2025_denmarkisation_agricultural_growth].
- Recovery: textile output +39% in 1933; H1 1934 "already firmly marked the start of a new economic upswing" — Karnups 2017, pp. 131–132. "The most difficult years were 1930, 1931 and 1932. By 1933 economic indicators suggested the beginnings of recovery"; Ulmanis "disingenuously used the perception of ongoing economic crisis" to justify the coup — Purs 2012, p. 124. Budget deficit −24.2 m Ls (1931/32) → −7.79 m (1933/34) — Karnups 2017, p. 131.

### 1.6 Ulmanis-era state capitalism and import substitution (1934–40)
- Coup 15 May 1934; "reasons were more political than economic"; regime drew on "autarky, state capitalism and corporate-state ideas" to create "a nationalised and national planned economy" — Karnups 2017, p. 134 [karnups_2017_karla_…]. Labels: Aizsilnieks "state-directed economy without a plan"; E. Krastiņš "bureaucratic state capitalism" — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 60 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…].
- Latvijas Kredītbanka founded 9 April 1935 (40 m Ls capital), absorbed seven banks in year one; 38 state joint-stock companies by March 1939 — Karnups 2017, pp. 143–144. By 1 Jan 1939 it had "taken over and transformed 31 commercial and industrial companies" (Laima, Riegert); "Latvianization of the economy and bringing the entire economy under state supervision were two sides of the same coin" — Wezel 2025, pp. 152–153. 1935 census: individual entrepreneurs per 1,000 of ethnic group: Latvians 24, Germans 32, Jews 53; output shares of collective firms 24% Latvian, 36% German, 35% Jewish — Krastiņš 2023, PDF p. 7 [krastins_2023_why_latvia_cannot]. Private banks 16 (1934) → 7 (1939) — Karnups 2017, p. 148.
- Slow slide to "state capitalism … most developed in Latvia, where the state controlled almost all facets of the economy by 1940" — Purs 2012, p. 124. 74 state/municipal industrial enterprises employed 23.6% of industrial workers in 1939; "rumours circulated that VEF faced bankruptcy" — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 86.
- VEF made radios, aircraft and the Minox; Vairogs built six aircraft; industrial employment 70,800 (1934) → 97,500 (1939) — Karnups 2017, p. 140. Ķegums HES: construction 20 Aug 1936, first 17.5 MW unit 15 Oct 1939, Swedish credit 11 m Ls; capacity 128.3 MW in 1939 — pp. 141, 161. Swedish capital built Ķegums, which met much of power demand "only because Latvia was still relatively un-electrified" — Purs 2012, p. 125.
- Cooperatives: "the advent of the authoritarian Ulmanis regime in 1934 spelled the death knell of co-operatives" — Karnups 2023, p. 34 [karnups_2023_ideology_development_latvian].
- Agricultural subsidies: de facto grain monopoly with prices "many times higher than world market prices"; 19.4 m Ls for export promotion 1934/35; state export companies (Bekona eksports, Sviesta un siera eksports) — Karnups 2017, pp. 135–136. Sugar: domestic cost 35–36 santims/kg vs 7–7½ in Hamburg — p. 137. Foreign farm labourers 28,000 (1934) → >46,000 (1938) — pp. 138–139.
- Trade 1935–37: Britain 35.8% and Germany 33.8% of exports; UK treaty 17 July 1934; 1932 German clearing — Karnups 2017, pp. 149–152. Trade surplus from 1937 — Wezel 2025, p. 134. Export structure 1939: timber 34.8%, butter 22.5%, pigs 10.6%, flax 10.6% — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 53.

### 1.7 1930s industrial growth and the prosperity-vs-stagnation debate
- Industry 1934–39 (enterprises / workers / output m Ls): 4,288/70,835/373 → 6,067/97,506/728 — Karnups 2017, p. 143 (Table 3) [karnups_2017_karla_…]. Industrial establishments 2,032 employing 44,100 (1923) → 5,977 employing 117,200 (1938), but average firm employed only 17 — Wezel 2025, p. 135.
- Karnups (stagnation): "even after the 15 May coup, the pace of Latvia's industrial development was slow" [Tomēr pat pēc 15. maija apvērsuma Latvijas rūpniecības attīstības tempi bija lēni] — Karnups 2017, p. 141; national income "grew slowly" [lēnām pieauga] from 857.6 m Ls (1933, 444 per head) to 1,256.0 m (1938, 637 per head): "the 1929 level was reached only in 1938" — pp. 148–149; "productivity fell… bureaucracy replaced economic self-initiative" — p. 145; by 1936 "The real economy did not grow" [Reālā ekonomika nepieauga] — p. 145; the "good years" ["labos gadus"] owed as much to world recovery and rearmament demand as to policy — pp. 161–162.
- Krūmiņš (growth, but not thanks to Ulmanis): GDP at 1935 prices 1,037.6 m Ls (1934) → 1,209.7 m (1939), +16.6%; industry +36.9%, agriculture +9.2% — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 80 (Table 1.7) [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…]; prosperity "was not the result of purposeful action by the Ulmanis regime" — p. 61; "Ulmanis's rule coincided with a period of economic upswing" — Krūmiņš 2017, p. 26 [krumins_2017_laika_posma_raksturojums_lidz_1940_…]. Stranga's critique: industry "grew mainly extensively and in small firms" for the protected home market — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 76–77.
- Purs: "the most thorough critiques strongly suggest … economic crisis would have been imminent if not for" WWII — Purs 2012, p. 125. Ulmanis's 17 March 1938 speech predicted 1938 as the cyclical peak — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 64–66.
- Aizsilnieks is the only émigré author with Latvian national-income data (1934–38) — Norkus 2016, p. 5 [norkus_2016_comparison_economic_growth]; semi-official national income 895.7 (1933), 961.0 (1934), 980.9 m Ls (1935); Reichskommissar Ostland series 857.6 (1933) → 1256.0 m (1938) — Norkus 2016, p. 15 (Table 2).

### 1.8 Living standards relative to Estonia, Finland, Scandinavia; education
- "In 1938–1939 Latvia's GDP per capita was the highest in the Baltics, lagging Finland and the average of the present-day EU (with the UK) by a mere 10%. Latvia has never been so close to the European average level in the past 100 years" [Tik tuvu Eiropas vidējam attīstības līmenim Latvija nav bijusi nekad pēdējos 100 gados] — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 60 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…]. "In 1935 only three European countries' average GDP per capita exceeded Riga's — Switzerland, Denmark and Great Britain… Latvia's average was 90% of the European average" — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 588 [krumins_2024_neapmaldities_nakotne_…].
- Roses & Wolf (as reported): Latvia 1,929 (1922), 2,798 (1929), 4,048 (1938) GK$1990; Estonia 3,771; Finland 3,589 (1938) — Norkus 2023, p. 123 (Table 8.2) [norkus_2023_…_output_growth_baltic]; Krastiņš 2023, PDF p. 10 ("Latvia's per capita GDP might have been around 65% of the level of Great Britain and 81% of that of Germany" in 1938) [krastins_2023_why_latvia_cannot]; Karnups 2022, p. 72 (Latvia 4,048 vs Lithuania 2,064 GK$1990: "nearly twice as high") [karnups_2022_latvian_lithuanian_economic].
- Norkus's own estimates (2011 int$): Latvia 3,834 (1913), 2,964 (1922), 3,766 (1929), 4,527 (1938); Finland 3,365 → 5,721; Estonia 3,341 → 3,796; Lithuania 2,650 → 3,619 — Norkus 2023, pp. 129–130 (Table 8.4). Hence parity with Finland "was not the case by 1938… Lithuania's GDPpc made up 63.3%, Estonia's 71.1% and Latvia's 79.1% of the Finnish figure", whereas "in 1913, Latvian output per capita still was above the Finnish value" — Norkus 2023, p. 154 [norkus_2023_…_baltic_economic_growth]. Interwar growth 1913–38: Latvia 0.67% p.a. — p. 151. "the lag separating them from Denmark and the other Nordic countries increased during the interwar independence period" — Norkus 2023, p. 134 [norkus_2023_…_output_growth_baltic]. Findings "do not support the narrative of this time as being an 'economic miracle'" — p. 145.
- Norkus et al. 2025: GDP pc growth 1913–38 Denmark 1.56%, Estonia 1.16%, Latvia 1.09%, Lithuania 0.93%; Latvia regained pre-war level "only in 1930"; 1930–38 Latvia 2.53% p.a.; 1938 food self-sufficiency ratio Latvia 1.16 vs Denmark 2.18 — Norkus et al. 2025, pp. 6–9 [norkus_et_al_2025_…]. The three states "looked like three more failures among many attempts to emulate Danish success" — p. 3.
- Clark (1938) 1925–34 means: Finland 138, Latvia 131, Estonia 127, Lithuania 80 int$1929 — Norkus 2016, p. 9 [norkus_2016_…]. Norkus 2016's first estimate: Latvia 2,500–3,000 GK$1990 in 1938, up from c. 2,100 in 1913 — p. 17.
- Real wages: skilled worker 1927, Riga = 100: Copenhagen 212, London 198, Stockholm 175, Berlin 129, Tallinn 87, Warsaw 78 (cost-of-living adjusted); a Riga worker "lived better than in many European big cities" — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 260 (Table 4.3) [krumins_2024_naudas_…]. Milk/meat consumption 1938: Latvia 566 l / 85 kg vs Denmark 417/57, UK 403/64 — Krūmiņš & Kļaviņa 2024, p. 319 (Table 5.2) [krumins_klavina_2024_sapnis_murgs_dzive_…]. But 1935: electricity in 2.35% of farms, piped water 0.38% — p. 309. "quality of life in Latvia was higher than in neighbouring countries" (hence foreign farm workers) — p. 316.
- Latvia–Estonia–Lithuania 1937: industrial output per capita 292 / 196 / 111 lats; electricity per capita 98 / 99 / 15 kWh; illiteracy 13% (1930) / 5% (1934) / 33% (1923) — Krastiņš 2023, PDF pp. 7–9 [krastins_2023_…]. 1897 literacy 78.4% (Vidzeme 94.6%) vs Russian Empire 21.1% — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 12–13 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…]. Life expectancy 53 (1924) → 60 (1938), "only c. 15 countries ahead of Latvia" — Mežs 2024, p. 206 [mezs_2024_…]. Vikšniņš counterfactual: without occupation "at least Finnish income levels would have been reached" — Smith et al. 2002, unpaginated, "WHAT WAS WRONG?" [smith_et_al_2002_baltic_states_estonia_ch_latvia_politics_1987].

## 2. Transition 1990–1995

### 2.1 Output collapse: the figures in circulation
- World Bank: real GDP −3.5% (1990), −8.3% (1991), "could have fallen by 30 percent in 1992"; industrial output Q3 1992 −34% y/y, projected −40% for the year — World Bank 1993, pp. 5, 7 [world_bank_1993_latvia_transition_market_ch_background_recent_economic]; p. 105 [world_bank_1993_latvia_transition_market_ch_industry]. "Latvia enjoyed one of the highest standards of living among the republics … per capita income almost 30 percent above the Union average"; 1990 GNP per capita USD 3,410 — pp. 4, 114 [..._background_recent_economic; ..._energy].
- Osis (official series): GDP −35% (1992), −15% (1993), +2% (1994); "the fall in GDP in just two years (1992–1993) reached 50%"; 1995 = 49.6% of 1990 (4,736.6 m lats at 1995 prices) — Osis 2017, pp. 390–391 [osis_2017_cenu_liberalizacija_inflacija_…]; p. 522 [osis_2017_uznemejdarbibas_attistiba_…]. Jeffries: −34.9% (1992), −14.9% (1993); EBRD 2001 GDP vs 1989: Estonia 90%, Latvia 75%, Lithuania 72% — Jeffries 2004, pp. 220–221 [Jeffries 2004 Latvia. In The countries of the former Soviet Union at the]. Åslund: "The total official fall in Latvia's GDP from 1990 to 1993 was no less than 49 percent" (figure label "−56 percent 1991–93"; Godmanis cited 37%) — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, pp. 7, 12, 39 [Aslund 2011 Latvia's post-Soviet transition…; …Policy choices…]. Bukovskis: "GDP contracted by 60 percent from 1990–1993"; GDP pc USD 1,743 in 1993 — Bukovskis 2015, p. 31 [bukovskis_2015_capital_latvia_notes]. Auers: −6.4% (1991), −32.9% (1992), −20.0% (1993) — Auers 2009, PDF p. 1 [auers_2009_…].
- Norkus (MPD 2020, 2011 int$): 15,806 (1990) → 13,775 (1991, −12.85%) → 9,411 (1992, −31.68%) → 8,439 (1993, −10.33%) → 8,709 (1995); 1989 level surpassed only in 2005 — Norkus 2023, pp. 161, 164 [norkus_2023_…_baltic_economic_growth]. Krūmiņš: 1993 trough 10,340.8 (2017 USD) vs 19,735.3 in 1986, "almost by half… a return to the 1960 level", GDP "only a third higher than in 1939"; decline began in 1987, by 1990 at 1973–74 level — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 115, 128 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…].
- Krastiņš: IMF data show 1992 fall "almost 40%" in Latvia vs 20% in Estonia and Lithuania; "by the year 2000 the volume of production had not even reached half the 1990 level"; Estonia regained 1991 GDP in 2002 — Krastiņš 2023, PDF p. 36 [krastins_2023_…].

### 2.2 Price liberalisation and inflation
- Price reform began 1991, "one year before… most parts of the former Soviet Union"; by mid-1992 <8% of CPI items controlled; subsidies 13.7% (1990) → 1.3% of GDP (1991); 1991 inflation 172.2% (262% Dec/Dec) — World Bank 1993, p. 4 [..._background_recent_economic]. Osis: "most significant price reform" 1 Jan 1991; "the most active year in price liberalisation was 1992"; "1993 was the year when almost all prices… had essentially been liberalised" — Osis 2017, pp. 377, 381–382 [osis_2017_cenu_…]. Jeffries: liberalisation "virtually complete by late 1992" — Jeffries 2004, p. 207.
- CPI 1991 180%, 1992 695.7% (services 2,723.6%), 1993 89.5%, 1994 33.5% — Osis 2017, p. 386; end-period 958% (1992) — p. 391. Alternative: 951.2% (1992), 109.2% (1993), 35% (1994) — Krastiņš 2017, p. 811 [krastins_2017_kreditiestades_…]; 959% (1992) — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, p. 9. Real wages −29% in 1991 — Kehris 2017, p. 534 [kehris_2017_nodoklu_budzeta_sistemas_…]. Over 95% of cash savings lost — Bitāns & Purviņš 2012, p. 153 [bitans_purvins_2012_development_latvia_economy].

### 2.3 The lats (1993)
- Latvian ruble from 7 May 1992 at 1:1; sole legal tender 20 July 1992; Bank of Latvia stopped buying non-convertible currencies 22 Jan 1993; 5-lats note 5 March 1993 at 1 Ls = 200 LVR; reform complete 1 July 1993 (taxes only in lats); 1993 lats appreciated 29% vs USD; pegged to SDR 12 Feb 1994 at 1 SDR = 0.7997 Ls, unchanged for ten years; re-pegged to euro 1 Jan 2005 at 0.702804 — Kehris 2017, pp. 462–473 [kehris_2017_naudas_reforma_monetara_…]. Repše: the LVR secretly tracked the SDR from H2 1992 — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 269–271 [krumins_2024_naudas_…]. Latvia had no currency board but "in practice pursued a quasi currency board" — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, pp. 8–9.
- Krūmiņš: "Estonia started reforms earlier and overcame the transformation crisis more successfully because it held parliamentary elections in 1992… (Latvia did so a year later)" — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 127 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…]; same point in Kehris 2017, p. 534 [kehris_2017_nodoklu_…].

### 2.4 Privatisation pace
- IMF reform index 1994: Latvia 0.71, Lithuania 0.79, Estonia 0.83; "Latvia lagged behind Lithuania and Estonia in terms of privatisation and trade liberalisation" — Bitāns & Purviņš 2012, p. 142 [bitans_purvins_2012_…]. Only 85 of 698 large enterprises privatised by April 1994; private sector 10% of GDP (1991), 40% (1994), 60% (1996), 70% (2001); Privatisation Agency founded 22 April 1994; voucher privatisation from Jan 1995; "The main bulk of enterprises were privatised in 1996-97" — Jeffries 2004, pp. 208–212; Smith et al. 2002, "LATVIA'S CONTINUING ECONOMIC CHALLENGES" [smith_et_al_2002_…_latvia_economy_since]. "in 1999, 81.5% of industrial turnover was generated in the private sector" — Osis 2017, p. 515 [osis_2017_uznemejdarbibas_…]. Počs: "The overall pace of privatisation was too slow"; agro-service privatisation was "'prikhvatisation'" — Počs 2015, pp. 120, 123 [pocs_2015_privatizacijas_procesa_nozime_…]. World Bank (2000) placed Latvia as leader in "state capture" — Jeffries 2004, p. 213. Krūmiņš: "better results… might have been achieved by orienting already in the early 1990s more towards attracting strategic foreign investors" — Krūmiņš 2017, p. 371 [krumins_2017_laika_posma_raksturojums_1990_2004_…].

### 2.5 Bank crisis 1995 (Banka Baltija)
- Banka Baltija offered 90% on lats deposits in 1993; c. 200,000 accounts and almost 200 m lats deposits by end-1994; suspended 22 May 1995, insolvent 27 June 1995 (loan portfolio pledged to Moscow's InterTek); 13 banks closed in 1995, 8 in 1996; closed banks held >42% of assets; "the direct effect of the bank crisis on GDP was in the range of 2–4%" [tiešā banku krīzes ietekme uz IKP bija 2–4% robežās]; money supply −17% in H1 1995 — Krastiņš 2017, pp. 811–815 [krastins_2017_kreditiestades_…]. Blocked assets >200 m lats = 40% of deposits, 53% of household deposits; broad money −23% — Bitāns & Purviņš 2012, p. 157. Åslund: 15 banks with 35–40% of assets closed; GDP −2.1% in 1995 — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, p. 11. Smith's "10 per cent drop in Latvia's GDP for 1995" is inconsistent with EBRD −0.9% — Smith et al. 2002, unpaginated; Jeffries 2004, p. 218. Fiscal cost of 1995–96 crisis 3% of GDP (vs Estonia 1992–94 1.6%, Lithuania 2.9%) — Krastiņš 2023, PDF pp. 41–42. Shadow economy rose from 30% (1995) to 42% (1996) "because of the bank crisis" — Kehris 2017, p. 540.

### 2.6 Deindustrialisation
- 1990: engineering 26.3% of industrial output, food 24.9%, light 18.0%; 90 All-Union enterprises; "only 46 percent of the enterprises in Latvia were controlled by the Latvian authorities"; >15% of the labour force in military production in 1985, "three times higher than in Estonia"; only 6.6% of output sold in the West (1991) — World Bank 1993, pp. 101–106 [..._industry]. Exports to former USSR 95.7% of total (1990) — Bitāns & Purviņš 2012, p. 141. Terms-of-trade loss >11% of 1990 GDP (Estonia ~13%, Lithuania ~10%) — p. 141; World Bank: 24% ToT fall, income loss 8–12% of GDP — World Bank 1993, p. 16 [..._macroeconomic_issues].
- VEF employed 19,489 (1986), Radiotehnika 14,054, Alfa 7,675, Kommutators 7,239; "socialist over-industrialisation" — Norkus 2023, p. 165 [norkus_2023_…_baltic_economic_growth]. Radioelectronics employed 50,000 and was "hit particularly hard" — Krastiņš 2023, PDF p. 35. "Electronics, telecommunications and automobiles are likely to disappear… VEF… is now defunct"; the "Latvija minibus" lines stopped — Smith et al. 2002, unpaginated. Krūmiņš: "a number of Soviet-era industrial flagships ceased to exist, of which the most striking example is VEF" — Krūmiņš 2017, p. 369 [krumins_2017_laika_…_1990_2004_…]. Manufacturing 44% of GDP (1991) → 10.6% (2008); agriculture 25% → 3.1% — Auers 2009, PDF p. 6. Osis's revisionist view: "Latvia's economy as a whole did not experience any collapse" [nekādu sagrāvi tomēr nepiedzīvoja] — Osis 2017, p. 522. Cellulose output 66.1 kt (1986) → 0 (1997) — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 440 [krumins_2024_latvijas_zalais_zelts_…]. Timber 37.3% of exports in 1999 — Remess 2017, pp. 569–570 [remess_2017_arejas_tirdzniecibas_politika_…]; cattle and pigs −70% in the 1990s — Krūmiņš & Kļaviņa 2024, p. 353.

### 2.7 Why Latvia's crisis was deeper than Estonia's
- Economy "deeply (more deeply than Lithuania and Estonia) integrated into the USSR economy, the territory more militarised, production controlled by all-Union ministries" — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 115 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…]. Godmanis's cabinet of Gosplan-trained professionals "without knowledge of a market economy"; "essential economic reform processes were delayed" — Krūmiņš 2017, pp. 365, 367 [krumins_2017_laika_…_1990_2004_…]. 1991 budget surplus >6% of GDP created "the illusion that radical reforms were not needed" — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 128. "The weakest part of Latvia's early postcommunist transition was deregulation" — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, p. 10. Latvia reached 62 banks (Estonia 22) under the Bank of Latvia's "almost libertarian stance" — Krastiņš 2023, PDF p. 41. Lithuania "did not figure so centrally in economic plans for rapid and heavy industrialization" — Purs 2012, p. 129.

### 2.8 Emigration of Russian-speakers
- 1992 emigration "almost 60 thousand persons… mainly to Russia"; 1990–2004 >350,000 left, c. 70% to former USSR republics — Mežs & Leiškalns 2017, p. 626 [mezs_leiskalns_2017_demografiska_situacija_sociala_1990_2004_…]. Soviet military c. 300,000 incl. families, 11% of population — World Bank 1993, p. 157 [..._housing_municipal_services]; troops withdrawn 31 Aug 1994, 22,000 military pensioners stayed — Jeffries 2004, pp. 182, 188. Ethnic Latvians 52% in 1989, 57.6% in 2000; non-citizens c. 740,000 (30%) in 1994, 515,000 (22%) in 2003 — Jeffries 2004, pp. 179–181. Population 2,668,140 in 1990 (all-time record) — Mežs 2024, p. 186.

## 3. Modern Latvia 1995–2025

### 3.1 Baltic tiger years and EU accession (2004)
- Growth 1996–2003: 3.7, 8.4, 4.8, 2.8, 6.8, 7.9, 6.1% — Jeffries 2004, p. 218. 1998 Russian crisis: GDP growth 6.3% (1998) → 2.2% (1999); exports to CIS fell 4.3× 1997–2000 — Krastiņš 2017, p. 818; Remess 2017, p. 573. "from 2000 to 2004 average GDP growth was 7.1%, while from 2005 to 2007… 10.8%" — Dombrovskis 2017, p. 671 [dombrovskis_2017_tautsaimnieciba_monetara_politika_…]. "none experienced higher growth than Latvia" 2000–07 — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, p. 118.
- EU application 13 Oct 1995; Luxembourg 1997 invited Estonia but not Latvia "(which at that time showed faster economic growth)"; talks from Feb 2000; referendum 20 Sept 2003; accession 1 May 2004 — Remess 2017, pp. 646–657 [remess_2017_cels_eiropas_savienibu_…]. At accession Latvia's GDP pc (PPP) was 39% of EU15, "the very lowest in the group of new member states" (Estonia 47, Lithuania 44); 1996: Latvia 27, Estonia 32, Lithuania 32; average wage EUR 303 vs Estonia 456, Lithuania 369 — pp. 653–655. "Latvia's accession to the EU had clearly illuminated its position as the poorest EU economy" — Bitāns & Purviņš 2012, p. 165.
- Boom: credit 18% of GDP (2000) → 88% (2007); current-account deficit 22.5% of GDP in 2006–07; inflation peak 17.9% May 2008; real-estate prices +240% 2003–07; investment 40% of GDP; public debt 9% of GDP end-2007 — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, pp. 19–25 [Aslund 2011 The boom, 2004-07…]. Kalvītis's "seven fat years" [Treknie gadi] address — Krūmiņš 2017, p. 663 [krumins_2017_laika_posma_raksturojums_es_nato_…]. Wages +16.5% (2005), +23.0% (2006), +31.5% (2007) — Dombrovskis 2017, p. 678. "Why produce if there is real estate?" [Priekš kam ražot, ja ir nekustamais īpašums?] — p. 679.

### 3.2 The 2008–10 crisis and internal devaluation
- "Measured as decline in GDP from peak to nadir—25 percent—Latvia experienced the most severe financial crisis in the world during the crisis years of 2008–10"; unemployment peak 20.7% (Q1 2010) — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, p. 1 [Aslund 2011 Introduction…]. GDP −3.6% (2008), −18.4% (2009), −0.2% (2010); IMF had forecast −5% for 2009 — p. 44. Hazans: end-2009 GDP 24% below pre-crisis maximum; legal employment −1/4 — Hazans 2013, p. 66 [hazans_2013_emigration_latvia_recent_…]. Revised series: 2009 −14.3% (CSB); "according to current (October 2016) Eurostat data… Latvia experienced the smallest recession: 14.3% in Latvia, 14.8% and 14.7% in Lithuania and Estonia" — Dombrovskis 2017, p. 689. MPD: 21,042 (2007) → 17,140 (2010), −18.5% peak-to-trough; 2007 level regained only in 2014/15 — Norkus 2023, p. 161. Property prices −60% in 2009, "the largest decline world-wide" — Auers 2010, PDF p. 7.
- Parex (second-largest bank, c. 14% of assets, EUR 975 m syndicated loans) nationalised Nov 2008; "Parex's problems became a special factor that made the situation in Latvia harder than in the other Baltic states" — Dombrovskis 2017, pp. 683–684. EUR 7.5 bn package = 37% of GDP, only EUR 4.5 bn used; public debt 42% of GDP end-2010 — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, pp. 2, 101. Fiscal consolidation "about 17% of GDP" in three years, six packages — Dombrovskis 2017, p. 712; World Bank estimate 13.3% of GDP 2008–10 — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, p. 106; IMF: gross adjustment 2009 13.9% of GDP Latvia, 8.8% Estonia, 8.0% Lithuania — Åslund 2013, p. 337 [Aslund 2013 The global financial crisis…]. Public wages −26% Nov 2008–Nov 2009; civil servants −30%; 35 of 59 hospitals closed; 115 schools closed — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, pp. 73–75, 108–109 [Aslund 2011 Implementation…; …The healing begins…].
- Devaluation debate: Krugman "Latvia is the new Argentina" (15 Dec 2008); c. 90% of loans in foreign currency; devaluation "would have to be large—perhaps 50 percent" — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, pp. 52–55 [Aslund 2011 To devalue…]. Current account swung from −13% (2008) to +9.4% of GDP (2009), "no less than 22 percent of GDP in one single year" — Åslund 2013, p. 346. "The causes of Latvia's crisis were entirely financial" — Åslund & Dombrovskis 2011, p. 102.
- Åslund's lessons (pp. 118–120 [Aslund 2011 Latvia's lessons…]): devaluation "never necessary or inevitable"; euro accession as nominal anchor; front-load cuts ("Latvia succeeded because it concentrated the fiscal adjustments in the first eight months"); expenditure cuts over tax rises; large front-loaded rescue; democracies can cut "about one-tenth of GDP in one year"; "populism is not very popular in a serious crisis"; "Three years of boom (2005–07) with a total growth of 33 percent were erased by three years of bust (2008–10), with a decline of 25 percent" — p. 121. Dombrovskis's counterfactual: with the euro in 2008 "the ECB would have provided Parex with liquidity" — Dombrovskis 2017, p. 719. Critics: Rostovskis "we did not use the opportunity of the 2008–2009 crisis"; Rungainis: Lithuania borrowed in private markets while Latvia went to the IFIs — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 582–583 [krumins_2024_neapmaldities_…].
- Euro adopted 1 Jan 2014 at 0.702804 Ls; "for Latvia the road to the euro turned out considerably longer… than for both Baltic neighbours" (Vilks) — Dombrovskis 2017, pp. 719–721. Exports +13.4% (2010), +12.0% (2011), +9.8% (2012); deficit 9.0% (2009) → 0.8% (2012) — p. 713.

### 3.3 Emigration and demographic decline
- "Since the beginning of the 21st century, Latvia has lost 9.1% of its population (including almost 14% of its working-age population)… Three quarters of adult emigrants are younger than 35" — Hazans 2013, p. 66. Net emigration 2000–10: official 33,100 vs Hazans 194,500–212,000 (8.2–8.9% of population); 2011 census found 348,000 fewer than published — pp. 68–69 (Table 4.1). Annual net emigration of nationals: 18.9k (2005), 29.2k (2009), 42.3k (2010), 30.3k (2011); 217,000 over 2000–11, UK 104.6k, Ireland 32.8k — p. 71 (Table 4.3). Latvia the only EU8+2 country where the recession raised net migration to EU15 — p. 67. Long-run GDP effect of a 2.5% outflow 3.3% (Holland et al.) — p. 101.
- 1991–2014 "almost 560 thousand residents left Latvia", net loss >413,000; emigration "approached the 40-thousand mark each year" in 2009–10; about every fourth person aged 20–39 emigrated — Mežs & Leiškalns 2017, pp. 761–762 [mezs_leiskalns_2017_…_2004_2014_…]. Population decline since 1990 "has already reached 800 thousand or about 30%"; mid-2024 1.86 m, lower than in 1900; Eurostat projects 1.27 m in 2070, with Estonia overtaking Latvia within 40 years — Mežs 2024, pp. 192, 213, 227. OECD: population "peaked at 2.7 million in 1990, reaching 1.9 million in 2020"; "a fairly strong likelihood that the population will have halved in the 70 years following its peak"; old-age dependency 35.5% (2020) — OECD 2022, p. 36 [oecd_2022_oecd_economic_surveys_latvia_ch_key_policy_insights]. Riga fell 18% since c. 2000 "from the Baltic/Nordic region's largest city… to fifth" — p. 38. Diaspora >370,000; >50,000 remigrated in nine years — OECD 2024, p. 63 [oecd_2024_…_key_policy_insights]. "since 2021 immigration has exceeded emigration" — Mažeiks 2024, p. 458 [mazeiks_2024_latvijas_cilveki_pasaule_…]. Latgale population 624,000 (1935) → 241,000 (2024) — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 83.

### 3.4 Productivity, investment, inequality, regions (OECD surveys)
- 2015: "In 2013, GDP per capita was 55% below the best performing OECD countries"; "1½ generations would be needed to catch up"; working-age population to fall 16% by 2060 — OECD 2015, p. 14 [oecd_2015_…_assessment_recommendations]. Middle-income-trap warning: "At around 15 000 USD per capita, the country's income is indeed close to one of the thresholds of the middle-income trap identified by Eichengreen et al. (2013)" — OECD 2015, p. 85 [oecd_2015_…_raising_productivity_ensuring]. Austerity 17% of GDP; domestic demand cut "by about one third" — pp. 16, 22. Shadow economy c. 24% of GDP, "8-9 percentage points higher than in Estonia or Lithuania" — OECD 2015, p. 72 [oecd_2015_…_improving_public_sector_efficiency]. R&D 0.6% of GDP; social spending 15% vs EU 28%; 35% at risk of poverty — OECD 2015, pp. 14, 27, 40.
- 2017: the income/productivity gap "also remains larger than in other Baltic countries or Central European countries" — OECD 2017, pp. 14–15 [oecd_2017_…_assessment_recommendations]. Riga metro = 69% of GDP; GDP pc "almost three times higher than in… Latgale"; Riga metro "performed less well than its peer cities in Estonia and Lithuania" since 2010 — pp. 45–46; OECD 2017, p. 111 [oecd_2017_…_making_most_economic]. GVC value added per worker "among the lowest in OECD countries"; 60% of goods exports resource-intensive — OECD 2017, pp. 68–72 [oecd_2017_…_moving_up_global]. Life expectancy 74, six years below OECD — p. 15.
- 2019: convergence continues "at a slower pace than before 2008"; productivity gap "larger than for other Baltic or Central European countries"; slowdown due to "a deceleration in capital deepening, as investment was subdued" — OECD 2019, pp. 15, 66 [oecd_2019_…_key_policy_insights; oecd_2019_…_policies_stronger_productivity]. ABLV (then third-largest bank) failed 2018; non-resident deposits −60% — pp. 28–29. Latgale and Vidzeme GDP pc "less than 40% of what it is in Riga" — p. 45. Business R&D 0.14% of GDP — p. 95.
- 2022: GDP pc "rose from 62% of the OECD average in 2015 to about 71% in 2021"; 2020 gap 22.5% to OECD average, "nearly double… to the top half"; catch-up "at a slower pace than in neighbouring Lithuania"; hourly productivity gap to top half c. 40% — OECD 2022, pp. 15, 17, 67 [oecd_2022_…_key_policy_insights; oecd_2022_…_boosting_productivity_incomes]. Reasons: "relative unit labour costs have risen more quickly than in all other OECD countries, except Iceland"; specialisation in "mature and relatively low-complexity products"; exporters "relying on low labour costs rather than innovation" — pp. 17, 70. Gini 34.5 (2020); "second-highest (relative) old-age poverty rate in the OECD" — pp. 15, 41. Regional GDP pc 171% (Riga) vs 47% (Latgale) of national average; at-risk-of-poverty >40% in Latgale — p. 39. GERD c. 0.7% of GDP, "about a quarter of the EU average" — p. 98. Venture capital "at least 10 times smaller than in Estonia" — p. 74. GDP −3.8% (2020), +4.8% (2021) — p. 18.
- 2024: "A deceleration of the income convergence process in the aftermath of the global financial crisis"; "Since then, growth rates have been much lower than before the crisis"; "Weak investment and skilled labour shortages exacerbated by a shrinking population have weighed on potential growth" — OECD 2024, p. 17 [oecd_2024_…_key_policy_insights]. GDP +3.0% (2022), −0.3% (2023), 1.8% (2024 proj.); HICP 17.2% (2022), 9.1% (2023); real wages −13% in Q3 2022 — pp. 20, 25. Shadow economy >20% of GDP; unreported wages c. 25% — pp. 34, 53. SOEs 7% of workforce, "triple the OECD average" — p. 49. "About two-thirds of non-financial enterprises… have no access to credit"; real lending rates "among the highest in the euro area"; stock-market capitalisation "the smallest in the whole euro area" — OECD 2024, pp. 85–87, 102 [oecd_2024_…_raising_investment_support]. Defence to 3% of GDP by 2027; debt c. 47% of GDP — pp. 29, 32. EIU: growth 0.3% (2023), 2.5% (2024), inflation 9.2% (2023) — EIU 2023, pp. 3, 8–9 [eiu_2023_country_report_latvia].
- Regional: Riga 46.5% of GDP (1996) → 58.2% (2004); Latgale GDP pc 50% of national in 2004, "lagging the Riga figure by 3.4 times"; capital-city premium rose 28 pp in Latvia (Riga 142 → 170) vs Harju 141 → 152 — Remess 2017, pp. 594–601, 613–614 [remess_2017_regionala_ekonomiska_attistiba_1990_2004_…]. Riga 2004: 82% of EU average; Riga/Latgale ratio 3.4× (2004) → 2.9× (2014); "State regional policy… has been insufficient" — Remess 2017, pp. 744–753 [remess_2017_regionala_…_2004_2014_…]. Labour market "'polar' — Riga and all the rest of Latvia" — Remess 2017, p. 602.

### 3.5 Krastiņš: why Latvia cannot catch up; falling behind after 2008 and 2019
- Question: "why has Latvia gone from being the Baltic leader in 1938 to a laggard? … the lag was already apparent immediately after regaining independence, and has become even more pronounced after the financial crisis of 2009" — Krastiņš 2023, PDF p. 21 [krastins_2023_why_latvia_cannot]. Thesis: "the main obstacle… is the culture of society deformed during the occupation"; a "bicommunal state" — pp. 51–52. Evidence: WWII population loss 22% (Latvia) vs 14% (Estonia, Lithuania), "It is very likely that it was Latvia that suffered the greatest losses… in terms of the quality of those who left" — p. 25; 1959 purge of national communists opened "the door to immigration and russification" — pp. 29–30; shadow economy 25.5% (2020) vs Lithuania 20.4%, Estonia 16.5%; CPI rank 2021 Estonia 13, Lithuania 34, Latvia 36 — pp. 17–18; banking restructuring cost 2.5% of GDP (Estonia 1.4, Lithuania 1.7); 1995 crisis 3%, 2008–12 3.9% of GDP; "Estonia and Lithuania, unlike Latvia, did not try to establish a financial centre to serve non-residents" (ABLV 2018) — pp. 41–43; Latvian PISA 2018 479/496/487 vs Estonia 523/523/530 — p. 16; trust: "most people can be trusted" 22% Latvia vs 34 Estonia, 32 Lithuania — p. 19.
- Krūmiņš 2024: 2005–25 "closer to stagnation"; GDP pc 19,000 → 33,000 (2017 USD) 2004–22, but total GDP 2022 <7% above 2007 (0.5% p.a.); Hansen: at Lithuania's growth rate since 2006 GDP would be EUR 9.8 bn higher; "Estonia overcame the economic transformation of the early 1990s more successfully, while after the 2008 crisis Lithuania has developed most successfully" — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 543, 132 [krumins_2024_neapmaldities_…; krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…]. R&D 0.75% of GDP (2022) (Binde) — p. 561. Endziņš: "we went step in step with the Lithuanians and Estonians until the financial crisis… After the financial crisis a scissors appeared in practically all economic indicators"; Rungainis: "since 2008 the Lithuanians have also passed us as if we were standing still" [kopš 2008. gada arī lietuvieši mums aizgājuši garām kā stāvošiem], "possibly for the first time in a thousand years" — pp. 571, 577–579. Reasons offered: linear cuts without structural reform in 2009, IFI borrowing, late break with Russia ("only after 2014"), zero tax on reinvested profit only 2017–18 vs Estonia, over-regulation, weak universities, lack of long-term strategy — pp. 538, 547, 582–585. Kazāks: NPLs peaked 22% in Latvia vs 8% in Estonia — Dombrovskis 2017, pp. 705–706.
- Bukovskis: 2019 GDP pc 69% of EU-27 (Latvia) vs 84% (Estonia, Lithuania); "the competition between the Baltic states to date has left Latvia side-lined"; accumulated investment in production 21.4% (Latvia) vs 47.6% (Estonia), 31% (Lithuania); key problems "demographic decline and emigration, middle income trap, low productivity growth" — Bukovskis 2022, PDF p. 7 [bukovskis_2022_four_stages_state]; Bukovskis 2015, pp. 31, 34–35 [bukovskis_2015_capital_latvia_notes]. Dombrovskis (2024): GDP pc rose from 42% to 71% of EU average over 20 years of membership; EU funds EUR 17.6 bn — Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 133–135 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…].

## 4. Baltic comparison and the Latvian relative decline

### 4.1 Richest before 1940 (and in 1989), poorest now
- "In 1938–1939 Latvia's GDP per capita was the highest in the Baltics" — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 60 [krumins_2024_makroekonomiska_…]; "in 1940… Lithuania's GDP per capita was almost two times smaller than Latvia's" — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 584 [krumins_2024_neapmaldities_…]. 1913: Estonia and Lithuania = 87% and 69% of Latvia; 1938: 83% and 80% — Norkus 2023, p. 134 [norkus_2023_…_output_growth_baltic]. 1989: "Latvia was the richest Baltic republic with 2844 roubles… per capita of national income. Estonia (2780) was at near parity", Lithuania 2584 — Norkus 2023, p. 156 [norkus_2023_…_baltic_economic_growth]; 1956 national income per capita Latvia 647 rubles, Estonia 636, Lithuania 459 — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 122. 1965: Estonia 144.75%, Latvia 140.27% of USSR mean — Norkus 2023, p. 157.
- "Latvia faced perhaps the largest challenges again, indicated by displaying the slowest recovery and the loss of its top rank according to output per capita among the Baltic countries, which it had in the interwar and last Soviet-era years" — Norkus 2023, p. 165. New north–south gap: by 2000 Latvia 67.3% and Lithuania 64.3% of Estonia's GDP pc — p. 164. MPD 2018: Estonia 27,409, Lithuania 27,371, Latvia 24,313 (2011 int$) — Norkus 2023, pp. 131–132 [norkus_2023_…_output_growth_baltic]. "Latvia and Lithuania are the poorest countries in the EU" — Purs 2012, p. 152. Krastiņš 2023, PDF p. 21 (quoted in 3.5). Latvia "the most industrialized Baltic state" — World Bank 1993, p. 101 [..._industry]; "Latvia was the most industrialized of the three Baltic republics" — Morrison 2001, PDF p. 3 [morrison_2001_…].
- Latvia's early rise and decline is compared by Norkus with Argentina, Uruguay and Chile — Norkus 2023, p. 144.

### 4.2 Norkus's growth comparisons and Finland as yardstick
- Finland included because interwar observers treated it as a Baltic state, it is "the 'future we have lost'", and its series are reliable — Norkus 2016, pp. 2–3 [norkus_2016_…]. Finland 1913–38: 2.15% p.a. (2,111 → 3,589 GK$1990), "the most economically successful Baltic country" — p. 18. Finland 1948–90 "a bit like… another 'Soviet republic with a capitalist economic system'", so the gap measures the depressing effect of socialism — Norkus 2023, p. 154 [norkus_2023_…_baltic_economic_growth]. 1989: Lithuania 54.4%, Estonia 57.3%, Latvia 58.0% of the Finnish level — p. 154. Finnish test for 2040: strict = parity; permissive = restore 1938 ratios; OECD forecast gives Estonia 86.7%, Latvia 81.5%, Lithuania 87.5% of Finland in 2040 — p. 174.
- "Growth rates of the restored Baltic countries in 1989–2018 were much higher than in 1913–1938… Estonia 0.79% [vs] 1.99%… Latvia 0.67% and 1.53%, Lithuania 1.25% and 2.17%" — Norkus 2023, p. 151. Recovery to 1913 level took "at least 17 years"; recovery of 1989 level: Estonia 2000, Lithuania 2004, Latvia 2005 — p. 164. 1999–2008 "the decade of most rapid growth… from the entire economic history of the Baltic countries (with a possible exception for the 1950s)" — p. 164. 2011–18 compound growth Estonia 3.19, Latvia 4.04, Lithuania 4.40% — p. 162. Success criterion: catching up with the EU mean (c. 70% of US) by 2040/50 = ultimate success — Norkus 2023, p. 52 [norkus_2023_…_performance_success_criteria].
- Norkus's explanation of Latvia's relative decline after 1991: "socialist over-industrialisation" (VEF etc.), the failed "'near Switzerland'" strategy (Banka Baltija 1995, Parex 2008 — "a cost Estonia and Lithuania avoided since their banks were foreign-owned"), Riga as "Baltic Paris" with a Russian-speaking entrepreneurial class, timber 30–40% of exports ("detour from the semi-periphery to the semi-periphery") — Norkus 2023, pp. 165–172.
- Krūmiņš: "Latvia's growth rate remained sufficiently high, considerably higher than in neighbouring Lithuania, and also ahead of Estonia" (interwar) — Krūmiņš 2024, p. 53; Latvian productivity below Scandinavia but above Lithuania and Poland (rye 12.5 vs Denmark 17.4 cnt/ha) — p. 52. Krūmiņš: "By the late 1960s Latvia's GDP per capita had approached 80% of today's EU GDP" — p. 113; and "Already in 2005 the highest GDP per capita level reached during the Soviet occupation (1986) was exceeded" — p. 132. Life expectancy higher than Finland's in 1960, then Finland pulled 4–5 years ahead — Mežs 2024, p. 207.

### 4.3 "Belgium of the East" and comparability with Finland/Scandinavia
- "Contemporaries perceived it as the 'Belgium of the East' (Hiden and Salmon 1991: 76–77)" — Norkus 2023, p. 135 [norkus_2023_…_output_growth_baltic]; also Norkus 2016, p. 16. Latvia "is believed to have been the second most affected country after Belgium" in WWI — Krastiņš 2023, PDF p. 6. Pre-1914 railway density per inhabitant greater than Germany and Belgium — Krūmiņš & Šiliņš 2017, p. 51. No other source in the corpus uses the phrase (Karnups, Wezel, Purs, Åslund, World Bank, OECD checked).
- Claims of comparability: Krūmiņš 2024, pp. 60, 588 (10% below Finland; Riga above Netherlands, Belgium, France, Sweden); Krūmiņš & Kļaviņa 2024, p. 318 ("taking the cost of living into account, a Riga resident lived better" than in Vienna, Rome, Prague, Warsaw); Krūmiņš & Šiliņš 2017, p. 42 (1913 industrial output comparable to Finland). Counter-claims: Norkus 2023, p. 154 (79.1% of Finland in 1938); Norkus et al. 2025, p. 3 ("three more failures"); Purs 2012, pp. 138–139 (Finland "witnessed more pronounced industrialization and modernization"); Norkus 2016, p. 1 (Kirby's question whether Balts "had enjoyed a prewar living standard comparable to that of the Finns"). Lehti: interwar statehood "elevated to the status of a new Golden Age" in post-1991 narratives — Lehti 2005, p. 92 [Lehti 2005 Estonia and Latvia - A new Europe challenges the old In D. J].
